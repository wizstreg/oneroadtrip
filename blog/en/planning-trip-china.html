Planning a Trip to China: Everything You Need to Know Before You Go

Published in May 2026 · Reading time: 22 min

Photo: Willian Justen de Vasconcellos / Unsplash

China is one of the most rewarding travel destinations in the world. It is also the one that requires the most preparation before you board. The reason is simple: the digital tools you rely on every day at home, like Google, WhatsApp, Maps, Instagram, and your Visa or Mastercard at the corner shop, are either blocked, useless, or rarely accepted. None of this is insurmountable. But none of it can be figured out on the fly once you land in Shanghai or Chengdu.

This guide walks you through what to install on your phone, how to pay, how to get around, what to expect on your plate, how to dress, what paperwork to prepare for health and insurance, how to read the weather across a country the size of a continent, and what to book ahead. It is written for the independent traveler heading to mainland China for the first time, or for someone going beyond the well-trodden Beijing and Shanghai loop into the inland provinces.

It is not a glossy promise that everything is easy. China takes preparation. But once that preparation is done, the country opens up in ways few other destinations can match.

Article written in May 2026. Visa rules, working VPNs, and payment apps change quickly in China. Always verify the latest conditions on official sources before you leave. Last update: 26 May 2026.

Visa and entry

The good news is that 2026 is the most open China has been to short-stay tourism in decades. As of early 2026, citizens of 46 countries can enter mainland China visa-free for up to 30 days for tourism, business, family visits, or transit. The exemption has been extended through December 31, 2026.

Major nationalities currently covered by the 30-day visa-free policy include: France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland, Austria, Portugal, Greece, Ireland, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, Poland, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Brazil, Argentina, Chile, and several others. The United Kingdom and Canada were added on February 17, 2026, also valid until end of 2026.

If your passport is not on the visa-free list, you have two other paths to enter without a traditional tourist visa:

  • 240-hour visa-free transit: citizens of 54 countries (including the United States) can stay up to 10 days when transiting to a third country through 60+ designated ports of entry. You must show an onward ticket to a country other than your departure country.
  • Hainan Island: 59 nationalities, including US passport holders, can visit Hainan visa-free for 30 days, provided they arrive directly from outside mainland China.

For longer stays, work, study, or journalism, a proper visa is still required. Apply through a Chinese Visa Application Service Center (CVASC) or consulate. Biometrics are mandatory.

📲 Digital arrival card (mandatory since 2024)

Every traveler entering mainland China must complete a digital arrival card 24 to 72 hours before arrival. The form is available on s.nia.gov.cn or through a mini-program inside WeChat or Alipay. Save the QR code as a screenshot, email it to yourself, and print a paper copy. Border staff will ask for it.

🏨 Police registration within 24 hours

Even with visa-free entry, you must register your address with the local police within 24 hours of arrival. Hotels do this automatically when you check in. If you stay in an Airbnb, with friends, or in a guesthouse that does not register you, go to the nearest police station yourself. This is not optional and travelers do get fined for skipping it.

Always verify the latest rules on the National Immigration Administration website (en.nia.gov.cn) and on your own country's embassy page before you book. Rules have changed every few months for the past two years.

Health and vaccinations

Visit a travel health clinic 4 to 6 weeks before departure. Routine vaccines should be up to date (tetanus, diphtheria, MMR). Recommended for China are hepatitis A and B, typhoid, and routine flu. Rabies and Japanese encephalitis are worth discussing if you plan to spend time in rural areas, especially in southern provinces during the wet season.

Air pollution can be serious in winter in northern cities (Beijing, Xi'an, Shijiazhuang) and parts of Sichuan and Chongqing. AQI readings above 200 are common on bad days, sometimes above 300. If you are asthmatic, sensitive, or traveling with children or elderly, pack a few FFP2 or N95 masks and check the air quality each morning on AirVisual.

Travel medicine kit to pack

Pharmacies are rare outside major cities, English-speaking staff rarer still. Bring a basic kit:

  • Loperamide (anti-diarrhea), 4 to 6 tablets. Use only when you have to move (long train ride, mandatory transfer). Otherwise, let it run its course and hydrate.
  • Ibuprofen or paracetamol for pain and fever.
  • Antihistamines for allergies.
  • Adhesive bandages, antiseptic (povidone iodine), a couple of sterile gauze pads.
  • Topical antibiotic (like Neosporin), hard to find locally.
  • Hydrocortisone cream sachets for skin reactions.
  • A small thermometer (useful with kids).
  • Tweezers.
  • Personal medications in original packaging with a translated prescription if possible.
🦠 Your gut needs time

Even seasoned travelers take weeks or months to adjust to the local microbiome. The first few days, stay strict: no ice in drinks (except major chains like Starbucks, KFC or McDonald's, which use commercial sterilized ice), no raw vegetables outside international hotels, fruit only if you can peel it, and food served piping hot. After a week, you can ease up gradually.

Altitude sickness matters if your itinerary touches the Tibetan plateau (northern Yunnan, western Sichuan, Tibet itself). Lhasa sits at 3,656 m and Shangri-La at 3,200 m. Diamox (acetazolamide) is available on prescription and helps with acclimatization. Climb slowly, hydrate, avoid alcohol the first two nights.

Tap water is not drinkable anywhere in China. More on that in the daily life section.

Travel insurance

Here is the part most travelers underestimate. China has no bilateral healthcare agreement with almost any country. Your home health card, your European EHIC, your Medicare, your NHS card, none of them cover anything in mainland China. Without travel insurance, you pay the full bill on the spot, immediately, via Alipay, WeChat Pay, or card.

Indicative costs at an international clinic in China:

  • GP consultation: 800 to 1,500 yuan (around $110-200 USD).
  • Appendectomy in Beijing at an international hospital (SOS, United Family, Parkway): around 120,000 yuan (around $17,000 USD).
  • Medical repatriation by air ambulance to your home region: 250,000 to 600,000 yuan ($35,000 to $85,000 USD) depending on distance and condition.

What to look for in a policy

  • Covers mainland China, not only Hong Kong and Macao.
  • Medical ceiling of at least $200,000 USD. $400,000+ for long trips.
  • Unlimited or high-cap medical repatriation ($500,000+).
  • 24/7 assistance line in English (or your language).
  • Maximum trip length covered, credit card insurance often caps at 90 days.

Common international options worth comparing include SafetyWing, World Nomads, IMG Global, Allianz Travel, Heymondo, and IATI. Whichever you pick, print the insurance card and 24/7 emergency number on paper. If your phone is lost or dead, you do not want to be searching for it from a hospital bed.

💳 Premium card insurance is rarely enough

Visa Premier, Mastercard Gold or Platinum cards often include travel medical coverage, but the ceilings (typically $150,000-$250,000) can be too low for a repatriation from a remote province. Always read the actual contract before relying on it for China.

Apps to install before you go

Install and test these before you board your flight. App stores can behave unpredictably from inside China, and some of these need a verification SMS to your home country number, which is easier to receive at home.

  • Alipay: universal payment app. Link your Visa or Mastercard before arrival.
  • WeChat: messaging, payment, mini-programs for everything. Set up payments at the same time.
  • VPN: install at least two. See the next section for current recommendations.
  • Apple Maps (iPhone only): works directly in China without a VPN, English interface, data sourced from AMap behind the scenes. The simplest option for iPhone users.
  • Amap (Gaode): the map app locals use, available in English as "Amap Global" on iOS and Android since 2024. Real-time traffic, ride-hailing integrated. More accurate than Baidu for rural areas.
  • Baidu Maps: deeper local POI database, but no English interface. Skip unless you read Chinese.
  • Pleco: Chinese-English dictionary with character recognition. Essential outside cities.
  • Hello Chinese: Duolingo-style app for learning the basics (pinyin, tones, greetings, numbers, places). A few hours before departure pays off enormously.
  • Google Translate: download the Chinese language pack for offline use. Camera mode translates menus and signs.
  • Microsoft Translator: backup, with a useful two-phone conversation mode.
  • Trip.com or Travel China Guide: for high-speed train and hotel bookings.
  • DiDi: the local equivalent of Uber, works in every city.
  • Dianping (大众点评): the Chinese Yelp, the reference for finding restaurants with reviews, ratings and discount coupons. Essential outside tourist zones. Interface is in Chinese, but the photos, ratings (out of 5) and price ranges are universal.
  • AirVisual (IQAir): real-time air quality.
🗺️ Why Google Maps does not work in China

Even with a VPN, Google Maps is unusable in China. The country uses an encrypted coordinate system (GCJ-02) different from the global standard (WGS-84). The offset between your actual position and what Google Maps displays is 100 to 500 meters. Useless for navigation. Use Apple Maps or Amap.

Pro tip: copy-paste the Chinese name (in characters) of your destination instead of the English translation. No ambiguity for the app, and you can show the screen to your taxi driver.

Payments and money

China has gone almost entirely cashless. Even in small villages, vendors hold up a QR code rather than a cash drawer. Visa and Mastercard are refused in 95% of shops. A few upscale hotels and international restaurants accept them, but counting on plastic at a noodle stand will leave you hungry.

The two apps that run everything

Alipay and WeChat Pay are the norm. Since 2023, both apps allow foreigners to link an international Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Amex, Diners Club, or Discover card directly. No Chinese bank account needed. Setup takes about 10 minutes per app:

  1. Download the app, register with your home phone number.
  2. Complete passport verification (a facial scan is part of it).
  3. Go to "Me > Bank Cards" and add your international card.
  4. Do a test payment of 10 yuan at a convenience store after you land to confirm it works.
⚠️ The old "Tour Pass" is gone

If you read older articles mentioning the Alipay Tour Pass, that prepaid product was discontinued in 2023. The new direct card-linking method is better: no deposit, no $200 cap, higher limits. There is still a Tour Card (a prepaid virtual card from Bank of Shanghai, accessible inside Alipay or WeChat) if direct linking fails for your bank, but most travelers no longer need it.

Cash, ATMs, and small tricks

  • Withdraw 500 to 1,000 yuan on arrival at the airport (ICBC, Bank of China, China Construction Bank ATMs accept foreign cards). That covers your first taxi, tips, and small purchases. Do not load up on cash, you will rarely need more.
  • Damaged, torn, or written-on banknotes are routinely refused. If a bank hands you scuffed bills, ask to swap them on the spot.
  • Hidden fees on big transactions: Alipay and WeChat apply a fee (around 3%) on payments above 200 yuan made with a foreign card. To avoid it, ask the vendor to split the bill into several payments under 200 yuan each. Most accept without blinking, it is a known local trick.
  • To scan a QR code in a restaurant or shop, always use the scanner built into Alipay or WeChat, not your phone's generic camera app. The camera will not trigger the payment flow.
  • No travelers checks. PayPal is not accepted in-store.

Internet and communications

Google, Gmail, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, X, international TikTok, Spotify, most Western news sites: all blocked without a VPN. This is the single biggest practical adjustment for first-time travelers.

VPN: install at least two, test them at home

The Great Firewall evolves constantly. A VPN that worked last month may not work this month. Based on tester reports in early to mid 2026, the most reliable options remain:

  • ExpressVPN, strong stealth servers, the most consistent on hotel Wi-Fi in tester reports.
  • NordVPN, obfuscated servers, post-quantum encryption.
  • Astrill, popular with long-term expats, dedicated China protocols.
  • Surfshark, NoBorders mode, no device limit.
  • Proton VPN, solid privacy-focused alternative.

Install at least two different VPNs before you arrive, on at least two devices. If one fails, you switch. Once inside China, downloading a new VPN from scratch is much harder.

📶 Common VPN trap on hotel Wi-Fi

Hotel Wi-Fi networks often have stricter firewall rules than mobile data. Your VPN may fail on the hotel network but work on your phone's data connection. Use your eSIM data for anything VPN-dependent (Gmail, WhatsApp, Google Maps tunneled through a VPN). Use hotel Wi-Fi for local streaming and routine work that does not need a tunnel.

eSIM: the easiest data option

For data, an international eSIM (Airalo, Holafly, Nomad, Trip.com) is the simplest path. It works the moment you land, no paperwork, no shop visits. The Trip.com eSIM gets consistently positive reviews from recent travelers. Buy it before departure.

A local Chinese SIM card requires passport registration in person, takes time, and is not worth it for trips under a month. Hotel Wi-Fi is fine for local Chinese sites and basic streaming.

Getting around China

The high-speed rail network

China operates the largest high-speed rail network in the world: over 45,000 km of dedicated track in 2025. Every provincial capital is connected. Commercial speeds range from 300 to 350 km/h. Trains run on time. Comfort is excellent.

Tickets open for sale a maximum of 14 days before departure, not earlier. Book through Trip.com, Travel China Guide, or directly on the official 12306 platform (Chinese interface, harder for non-readers). Your passport is scanned at the station entrance and again before boarding.

Chinese citizens pass through automated gates that scan their ID. Foreign passport holders usually need a manual check by a human agent. Look for the line marked "Manual" or "Foreign passports" at large stations.

  • Luggage: official limit is 20 kg per person, rarely enforced unless your suitcase is enormous. No hold compartment, bags go in racks above the seats or at the ends of carriages.
  • Station security: airport-style scanners at every station entrance. Aerosols, pocket knives, and lighters are sometimes confiscated, with rules applied inconsistently. Do not pack anything precious in carry-on that could be flagged.
  • Slow trains (Z, T, K, Y, S categories): an underrated option for long overnight journeys. You sleep on board (hard or soft sleeper berths) and save a hotel night. Toilets used to be rough, they have improved, but bring wet wipes.
🚌 High-speed rail vs long-distance bus

High-speed rail can be expensive on long routes. Long-distance buses are cheaper but much slower and less comfortable. Take the train for slow-travel comfort, take the bus only if budget is tight.

Three ways to plan your trip

Option A, Recommended

High-speed rail + local driver-guide on demand

Take the train between provincial hubs (Beijing → Xi'an, Chengdu → Kunming, etc.). Hire a local driver-guide only for the harder bits: scattered natural parks, terraced villages spread across 50 km, sites that require navigating dirt roads. Daily rate for a driver-guide is around 800 to 1,500 yuan (roughly $110-210 USD), often including the vehicle and fuel.

This is the best balance for independent travelers who want to keep momentum but offload logistics where it matters.

Option B

Private English-speaking driver-guide for the whole trip

One driver, one guide (often the same person), every day. They handle airport pickups, site tickets, restaurant choices, hotel check-ins. Insist explicitly on English-speaking, many "English-speaking" drivers know ten words. Daily rate similar to Option A, 800-1,500 yuan.

Ideal for first-time travelers, seniors, or anyone who wants zero logistics. Less spontaneous, but stress-free.

Option C

Full autonomy

VPN, Alipay, WeChat, Amap, Pleco. Doable for travelers experienced in Asia, much harder outside major cities. Foreigners cannot legally drive in China without a Chinese driving license. Car rental is effectively closed to short-term visitors. You will rely on trains, taxis, DiDi, and the metro.

Urban transport day-to-day

  • Metros are excellent in every major city, signage in Chinese and English. In Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, and others, you pay by tapping your phone (NFC) on the gate, via Apple Pay linked to Alipay, or via the metro mini-program inside Alipay or WeChat. Much faster than the QR code. Physical tickets are still available at machines (English menu).
  • Metro security: bag scanners at every station entrance, again like an airport. A faster trick at busy stations is to open the bag and show it to the agent rather than queue for the scanner. Liquids are sometimes checked (they may ask you to sip).
  • Taxis: cheaper than in Europe but few drivers speak English. Have your destination in Chinese characters ready (Pleco screenshot or copy-paste from Amap).
  • DiDi: the most reliable ride app. English interface, but the driver speaks Chinese. Payment is in-app, no cash.
  • Local buses: tricky without Mandarin, skip unless the route is obvious. NFC tap does not work on buses, you need a city-specific mini-program inside Alipay or WeChat, or exact change.

Climate and seasons by region

China stretches 5,000 km from east to west and through five climate zones. There is no single "best time to visit." It depends entirely on where you are going.

  • North (Beijing, Xi'an, Shanxi): hot dry summers (June-August, 30-35°C), bitterly cold dry winters (December-February, -5 to -15°C), spring sometimes brings sandstorms.
  • East and center (Shanghai, Hangzhou, Suzhou): hot humid summers (July-August, 35°C plus humidity), chilly winters (0-5°C), spring and autumn are ideal.
  • South (Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Hainan): subtropical. Very hot stormy summers, mild winters (15-20°C), typhoon season August-September.
  • Inland southwest (Hunan, Guangxi, Guizhou, Jiangxi): stifling in July-August, monsoon rains May-July, ideal in April or October.
  • Southwest and plateau (Yunnan, western Sichuan, Tibet): mild year-round in Kunming, but altitude means cold nights even in summer. Rainy season June-August.
  • Northwest (Xinjiang, Gansu): extreme continental climate. +40°C in summer, -20°C in winter, very dry.
🚫 Periods to avoid everywhere
  • Golden Week (October 1-7): massive domestic crowds, transport saturated, sites packed.
  • Chinese New Year (late January or February depending on the year): the largest annual human migration on Earth. Many shops close, transport is booked solid months ahead.
  • Qingming Festival (early April long weekend): smaller-scale crowds, but worth knowing.

On-the-ground weather and risks

The previous section tells you when to go. This one tells you what to do once you are there.

Weather and air quality apps

  • Amap (Gaode): precise local weather per neighborhood, in Chinese but visual.
  • Apple Weather / Google Weather: fine for cities, 10-day forecasts reliable except in mountains.
  • AirVisual (IQAir): real-time air quality, essential in winter in the north and in Sichuan. Displays AQI and protection recommendation.
  • Windy.com: for travelers heading to altitude (Tibet, western Yunnan, Fanjingshan). The European ECMWF model is available and more reliable than Chinese sources for incoming fronts.

Regional weather risks to know about

  • Typhoons (south and east coast, June-October): Hong Kong, Hainan, Fujian, Guangdong. Peak in August-September. Domestic flights are cancelled for 24-48 hours, high-speed rail usually keeps running. Signal T8 or above means the city stops. The Hong Kong Observatory (English) issues alerts for the whole south.
  • Monsoon (inland southwest, May-July): Hunan, Guangxi, Guizhou, Jiangxi, southern Yunnan. Short intense rain. Longji terraces look stunning but trails get slippery. Bring a light rain jacket rather than an umbrella.
  • Winter smog (north, December-February): Beijing, Xi'an, Shijiazhuang. AQI can spike above 300 (very unhealthy). Wear an FFP2 mask, consider limiting outdoor activity for sensitive travelers. Check AirVisual each morning.
  • Sandstorms (north, March-April): Beijing and beyond, wind from Mongolia. Occasional but spectacular. Sunglasses help.
  • Mountain fog and mist (high tourist sites): Huangshan, Lushan, Fanjingshan, Emei Shan. Mist can hide the view entirely for 2-3 days. It is also what makes these mountains famous in classical Chinese painting, a judgment call. Build a buffer day into your plan.
  • Extreme heat (inland south, July-August): the "four furnaces" (Chongqing, Wuhan, Nanjing, Nanchang) push past 40°C with high humidity. Hydrate constantly, loose clothing, AC pauses every 2 hours.
  • Dry extreme cold (north and northwest, December-February): Harbin, Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang. -20 to -35°C. Down jacket essential. Indoor heating in hotels and trains is strong, but the transitions are brutal.
⛰️ Altitude and exposure

At Lhasa (3,656 m), Shangri-La (3,200 m), the top of Huangshan (1,864 m) or Fanjingshan (2,572 m), weather changes fast and cold arrives quickly in the late afternoon even in summer. Always pack a thermal layer in your day bag.

If a typhoon or snow alert hits, local authorities will close natural sites without warning. Plan flexibly and prioritize must-see visits on favorable weather days.

Luggage and clothing

Travel light. Every experienced China traveler says the same thing. Train stations and airports are huge, walkways and stairs endless, taxis and DiDi cars not built for oversized luggage. The ideal setup: one carry-on backpack plus a small shoulder bag. If you are traveling for more than a month, add one hard suitcase that you can check in, but keep mobility as the priority.

  • Temple dress code: shoulders and knees covered in Buddhist and Taoist temples. Tibetan monasteries are stricter (long trousers, long sleeves, hats off).
  • Cities are very Western in dress code, no special outfit required.
  • If your trip spans multiple regions (Beijing + Yunnan in April, for example), pack a layering system. A warm coat and a t-shirt may both be needed in the same suitcase.
  • Good walking shoes are non-negotiable. Sites take hours to walk through, natural parks demand 4 to 6 hours of hiking.
  • Universal travel adapter, see the daily life section for the plug types.
  • Bring a paper copy of your passport, visa (or visa-free confirmation), and insurance, stored separately from the originals.

Food and eating out

Chinese cuisine is not a monolith. It is officially organized into eight great regional cuisines: Lu (Shandong), Chuan (Sichuan), Yue (Cantonese), Su (Jiangsu), Min (Fujian), Zhe (Zhejiang), Xiang (Hunan), and Hui (Anhui). Each has its own flavor profile, signature dishes, and cooking techniques.

A few orienting facts:

  • Hunan is the spiciest of all, more so than Sichuan. Sichuan has the famous tongue-numbing pepper (huajiao) on top of heat. Hunan goes for pure unadulterated chili.
  • Chinese breakfast is savory: noodle soups, steamed baozi buns, jianbing (crispy crepe-omelet). The sweet pastry breakfast is not a thing here.
  • Tap water is not drinkable. Bottled water is everywhere, 2-3 yuan a bottle. The most common reliable brand is Nongfu Spring (农夫山泉). Hot boiled water is free in every hotel, station, and train (thermos flasks everywhere).

Table etiquette that matters

  • Slurping noodles is welcome, it shows appreciation. Silent eating can read as disapproval.
  • Never stick chopsticks vertically into your rice. It mirrors a funeral ritual and is taken badly.
  • If serving chopsticks are provided, use them when picking from shared dishes (not your personal ones).
  • If the server brings a bowl of hot water with the cutlery, it is to rinse the dishes one final time before use. Do it, dry with a napkin, it is normal and a good sign.
  • Many restaurants serve dishes still wrapped in plastic film, washed and sterilized by an external service. Perfectly clean, do not be alarmed.

Vegetarians, vegans, allergies

  • Harder than in the West. The key word is 素食 (sùshí), vegetarian.
  • Many "vegetable" dishes contain meat broth. Adding 不要肉 (bú yào ròu), "no meat", helps.
  • For allergies, prepare a Chinese-language card listing your allergens. The Google Translate camera mode helps decipher menus.

Tipping is not practiced and can be unwelcome. Driver-guides on private tours are the exception.

What to book in advance

  • International flights: Air China, China Eastern, China Southern, plus most major airlines from Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Hubs are Beijing (PEK and PKX), Shanghai (PVG and SHA), Guangzhou (CAN), and Chengdu (TFU).
  • Domestic flights: useful for long crossings the high-speed rail does not yet cover well (Urumqi to Lhasa, Kunming to Beijing direct, etc.). Book through Trip.com, the airline's own site, or Ctrip. Internal flights are often cheaper than the equivalent train, but less punctual: delays of 1-3 hours are common, weather-related cancellations frequent in summer (typhoons in the south, thunderstorms in the southwest).
  • Hotels: Booking, Trip.com, and Agoda all work from outside and inside China.
  • High-speed rail tickets: open 14 days before departure. Book through Trip.com or Travel China Guide (English interface, small fee). The official platform is 12306, which now requires passport scan and ID verification for foreigners since 2024. Interface in Chinese, harder for non-readers but the lowest prices.
  • Major tourist sites (Forbidden City, Terracotta Warriors, parks like Zhangjiajie or Fanjingshan): mandatory online reservation via WeChat with passport details, often several days ahead in high season.
  • State museums: reservation via WeChat. Usually free, but daily quotas can be very low (Hunan Museum, Shanghai Museum).
🎟️ Foreign passport perks

Foreign passport holders often get free or reduced entry to state museums in China. Always show your passport at the ticket booth.

Etiquette and behavior

  • Business cards: hand and receive with both hands. Matters in B2B contexts.
  • Toasts at the table: stand for the most senior person's toast. Hold your glass slightly lower than your host's when clinking, a sign of respect.
  • Bargaining: standard in markets, not in regular shops. Divide by two then work back up.
  • Photos of people: ask first, especially when photographing ethnic minorities (Miao, Yao, Tibetan, and others).
  • Political topics (Tibet, Taiwan, Xinjiang, Tiananmen): best avoided in public conversation. Not worth the discomfort, for you or for the locals you put on the spot.
  • Noise: restaurants and trains are loud. Cultural norm, not rudeness.
🚇 Queueing, or not

European-style queueing is not universal in China, especially around buses, metros, and lifts. If you stand politely a meter back from the door, people will go around you. Stay at arm's length from the person in front of you, hold your ground. Always give way to the elderly and pregnant women.

  • Pushy street vendors: the magic phrase is bùyào (不要), "don't want." Short, clear, effective.
  • Temples and religious sites: no hats inside prayer halls, no photos of statues (signs are posted), walk clockwise around stupas and prayer wheels in Tibetan Buddhist monasteries.
  • Minimum Mandarin to learn before departure (via Hello Chinese): ni hao (hello), xie xie (thank you), bu yao (don't want), duo shao qian (how much), wo bu hui shuo zhongwen (I don't speak Chinese). Get familiar with pinyin and the four tones, place names live and die by their tones.
💆 A local pleasure to try

End-of-day foot massages are an institution. 30-80 yuan for 45 minutes, available everywhere in cities. After a day of walking through parks and old towns, it is a small revelation.

Safety

This may surprise you: urban China is one of the safest places to travel in the world, including at night. Counterintuitive coming from Europe or North America, but consistent across nearly every traveler report and crime statistic.

Mugging and street crime against foreigners is rare. Surveillance is omnipresent in cities, police presence is visible. The most common issues for tourists are not violent:

  • Scams: fake "art students" who invite you for tea (inflated bill at the end). Unofficial taxis at airport exits charging triple. Be polite, decline, walk away.
  • Solo female travelers: China is very practical for women traveling alone. Metro and DiDi rides feel safe day and night.
  • Standard precautions: paper copies of documents, do not flash valuables in dense crowds, watch your bag on packed metros.

Daily life: power, toilets, water, time

Electricity

220V, 50Hz. Three plug types coexist: Type A (two flat pins, US style), Type C (two round pins, EU style), and Type I (three flat pins in a V, Australian style). Most hotel rooms have hybrid outlets that accept several types, but do not count on it outside major cities. Bring a universal travel adapter.

Time zone

The entire country runs on UTC+8, despite spanning five geographical time zones. In Xinjiang, Tibet, and western Sichuan, this means the sun rises around 9 AM and sets near 10 PM in summer. There is no daylight saving, China does not change clocks.

Public toilets

  • Mostly squat-style outside upscale hotels and restaurants.
  • Toilet paper is often not provided. Soap often not either.
  • Always carry a packet of tissues and hand sanitizer.
  • High-speed train toilets: Western-style available, paper provided, generally clean.
  • Natural park toilets: basic. Bring your own supplies.

Water

Tap water is not drinkable, anywhere. Bottled water sells for 2-3 yuan everywhere. The most reliable common brand is Nongfu Spring (农夫山泉). Hot boiled water is free in hotels, stations, and trains. For ice: avoid it outside major commercial chains (Starbucks, KFC, McDonald's, big Chinese chains) that use commercially sterilized ice.

Emergency numbers and embassies

Emergency numbers in China (same across the country, dialable from any phone):

  • 110, Police
  • 120, Ambulance and medical emergencies
  • 119, Fire
  • 122, Road accidents
  • 12308, Chinese consular hotline for foreigners (English sometimes available)

Your embassy

Your home country's embassy in Beijing, or its consulate in Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Wuhan, or Shenyang, is your primary contact in case of arrest, serious hospitalization, lost passport, or disappearance. Find your nearest embassy at embassyworldwide.com/country/china or check your government's official travel advisory website before you leave.

Other useful contacts:

  • Your travel insurance 24/7 emergency line, print it on paper and tuck it inside your passport.
  • International SOS, for the list of medical facilities covered by your insurance.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a visa for China?

As of 2026, citizens of 46 countries (including most of Europe, the UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Brazil, and Argentina) can enter China visa-free for up to 30 days. US passport holders still need a visa for most travel, but can use the 240-hour transit scheme or visit Hainan Island visa-free. Always verify on the National Immigration Administration site (en.nia.gov.cn) before booking.

Can I use Google Maps in China?

No, even with a VPN. China uses an encrypted coordinate system that creates a 100-500 meter offset between your real position and what Google Maps shows. Use Apple Maps (iPhone, works directly) or Amap Global on iOS and Android.

How do I pay in China without Alipay or WeChat?

It is very difficult outside international hotels. Visa and Mastercard are refused in 95% of shops. The simplest solution is to link your international card directly to Alipay and WeChat Pay before arrival, both apps now accept Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Amex, Diners Club, and Discover.

Can foreigners rent a car in China?

No. Foreign driving licenses (including international permits) are not recognized for rental in mainland China. You need a Chinese driving license, which requires residency. Hire a driver-guide instead, or use the high-speed rail network.

Is China dangerous for travelers?

Urban China is among the safest destinations in the world, including at night. Violent crime against foreigners is very rare. The main risks are minor scams (fake art students, unofficial taxis) and pickpocketing in dense crowds, standard travel precautions apply.

Do I need to speak Chinese to travel in China?

No, but a few tools are essential: Google Translate (with the Chinese offline pack), Pleco for character recognition, and the basics from Hello Chinese (greetings, numbers, "I don't speak Chinese"). Outside major cities, English is rare.

Is Chinese food really that spicy?

Only in some regions. Sichuan and Hunan are intensely spicy (Hunan is the hottest of the two). Cantonese, Shanghainese, and northern cuisines are generally mild. Restaurants outside spicy regions will tone things down on request, ask for bú yào là (不要辣), "not spicy."

What is the time difference with my country?

China runs on UTC+8 year-round, with no daylight saving. The time difference depends on where you are coming from:

  • Western Europe (Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Rome): 6 hours ahead in summer (CEST), 7 hours ahead in winter (CET).
  • United Kingdom and Ireland: 7 hours ahead in summer (BST), 8 hours ahead in winter (GMT).
  • US East Coast (New York, Toronto): 12 hours ahead in summer (EDT), 13 hours ahead in winter (EST).
  • US West Coast (Los Angeles, Vancouver): 15 hours ahead in summer (PDT), 16 hours ahead in winter (PST).
  • Australia East Coast (Sydney, Melbourne): 2 hours behind in their summer (AEDT), 3 hours behind in winter (AEST). Yes, China is behind Australia.
  • India: 2.5 hours ahead.
  • Brazil (São Paulo): 11 hours ahead.

Jet lag going east (toward China) is generally harder than coming back.

Should I bring cash to China?

Withdraw 500-1,000 yuan on arrival for your first taxi and small purchases, then rely on Alipay and WeChat Pay. China is almost entirely cashless, even rural vendors prefer QR codes. Damaged banknotes are refused, so check the bills the ATM gives you.
