01

Welcome to Provence

The cicadas will lull you through the afternoon nap, which is more or less compulsory in summer, often in the shade of an umbrella pine and often by the sea or with your eyes fixed on a field of lavender.

Welcome to Provence, land of inspired painters, of former popes, of the horses and bulls of the Camargue, of hilltop villages and gorges with turquoise water.

Here the good wine will be drunk with petits farcis, stuffed vegetables. The bouillabaisse will be eaten at a table facing the Mediterranean or on Marseille's Vieux-Port. Candied fruit made by a centuries-old method will delight you. The old lanes lined with shops selling Provençal fabrics will welcome you once the nap is over. And the Papal Palace in Avignon will give up its secrets.

Here, the locals will come up to you without a second thought to ask where you are from, and their southern accent will sound like it walked straight out of a Marcel Pagnol novel, the local boy made good.

Welcome to Provence, land of the sun.

Provence is vast, very vast, and it cannot be covered in one go. So we shall begin with Marcel's country, where sea, sun, cliffs and hills meet: the Bouches-du-Rhône.

02

What to do in Marseille and around

The Bouches-du-Rhône is the sea. Everywhere, you will hear about the calanques, those narrow inlets where the sea pushes in between limestone cliffs. You will discover them by boat or, better, at the cost of a punishing walk under a beating sun. Go for June or September: in summer, access to the massif is often closed because of fire risk. The walk will be repaid by the view, when you reach the top of the hill above your first calanque. And the swim that follows will send you back stronger, because the climb is a hard one. People usually set off from Luminy, east of Marseille, but there are other entry points (you will find information here: From Marseille | Calanques National Park)

If you would rather see the calanques another way, less punishing but no less astonishing, take to the sea. Below is a trip rated 4.7 out of 5. Note that it lasts half a day, but you will get an eyeful and you will throw yourself into the warm Mediterranean water. Refreshing.

With Marseille as your base, you can easily reach the towns and villages around, by car or, better, by public transport.

In Aix-en-Provence, it is the Provençal markets, the fountains, the grand townhouses and Cézanne's light that await you. Aix is the opposite of Marseille, half an hour away. A quiet, well-heeled town, where you will recover from the Marseille racket in the shade of the plane trees on the cours Mirabeau. Do consider a guided tour, it is far better for understanding the spirit of Aix and the history of the magnificent private mansions lining the old lanes. It costs little and it saves you from going round in circles.

In Aubagne country, you will walk in Marcel's footsteps across Pagnol's hills. One thing to do if you possibly can is to join a Pagnol play (in French) that takes you through the hills to the mythical settings of his novels, with a scene performed at each spot. Careful, the cagnard, the blazing heat, can do real damage: water bottles and sun protection are not optional. For the dates of the performances, it is here. Dans la cour des grands

In December, head to Aubagne for its famous santon market.

On to Cassis, where you will be charmed by the gentle harbour and the shops in the old lanes.

From there, push on into the neighbouring Var as far as the village of Le Castellet, better known for its racing circuit than for its streets, and worth the visit all the same.

That leaves Marseille. The oldest city in France, founded by the Greeks more than 2,600 years ago, a cosmopolitan and restless place where the Bonne Mère has watched over the faithful for centuries, while at her feet the Mucem opens its doors to the cultures of the whole world.

As in Aix-en-Provence, we would advise a guided walk. You will learn far more than on your own and the price is not excessive.

Guided tour
Explore Marseille

If you would rather go it alone, be on the quai des Belges early in the morning, where the fishermen sell the day's catch right there on the quay.

From the same quay, a boat takes you in twenty minutes to the Château d'If, the fortress where Dumas locked up his Count of Monte Cristo. They still show visitors the cell of a prisoner who never existed. The boat carries on to the Frioul, two bare islands where you can swim in empty coves.

The Bonne Mère is nearby, above the port, on her hill. You get up there on foot, by bus, or on the little tourist train that leaves from the port. There was a funicular once, but it has been gone since 1967.

You come back down, then head up the Canebière, Marseille's main avenue, and cross old Marseille, the Noailles quarter and its market, where every nationality mixes. If you want to shop, the rue Saint-Ferréol is waiting for you: locals call it Saint-Phé.

Save the cours Julien for the evening, that is where Marseille comes to enjoy itself after dark. Instead, carry on to place Castellane, walk up the avenue du Prado and reach the Prado beaches. All of this can be done on foot, but reckon on a good few kilometres in your legs.

Then take the metro to the Panier, one of Marseille's old quarters, all uphill. And come back down to the Mucem before settling in at the cours Julien for the evening.

All right, that is over the top. Spread it over two or three days or you will not keep up. Among the things we have not mentioned: the whole seafront, the maison du fada (Le Corbusier's Cité Radieuse), the Palais Longchamp.

03

The other coast: L'Estaque, the Côte Bleue, Martigues

Now head for Marseille's westernmost quarter, L'Estaque and its harbour, painted again and again by Cézanne, Braque and Dufy. You will eat chichis fregis, sugared fritters, standing on the quay, and if you turn up on a Sunday you will see two men in white fighting it out with lances on rowing boats until one of them falls in.

Here begins another coastline, quite unlike the famous calanques. The best way to take it in is probably from the water, as with the calanques. If you feel up to it, hire a kayak for half a day. Magnificent scenery and good swimming await.

Boat trip
Kayaking on the Côte Bleue

The rocky coast is dotted with narrow inlets, spanned by the railway you will take from Marseille Saint-Charles to reach Niolon in twenty minutes. Avoid the car, often banned. At Carry-le-Rouet, a marine reserve has banned fishing since 1982: it is the one place around here where the fish watch you go by two metres from the shore. In February, the harbour fills up for the oursinades, the sea urchin festival, and the locals will talk to you about Fernandel before they talk to you about the sea.

Then the coast stops dead at Martigues. Here, the water comes into the town: three canals, pointus, the local wooden boats, moored at the foot of the houses. Locals compare their handsome town to Venice and will point you to the Miroir aux Oiseaux, a stretch of water dotted with small boats and lined with ochre houses with brightly coloured shutters, listed since 1942. The Ziem museum holds more than 8,000 works and makes for a fine, cool walk if the nap has not caught up with you.

04

Alpilles, Arles and the Camargue

Let us leave the Mediterranean and head north.

Here we are in the Alpilles, a chain of limestone hills between Arles and Avignon, wedged between the Rhône and the Durance. The white of the rock is tinted with the green of centuries-old olive trees and Aleppo pines. Here, the cicadas rule. You drive, and with every ridge you cross the landscape changes. The villages are discovered at Provençal pace: slowly in the morning, pastis at midday, a stroll in the late afternoon, and in summer the evening markets.

At Les Baux-de-Provence, the village clings to its rocky spur and its castle is nothing but a ruin. From the top, on a clear day, the eye reaches the Camargue. Just below, the Carrières de Lumières project the work of great painters onto the walls of a former stone quarry: you walk into the dark and come out with your eyes full of images and your ears full of classical music. All around, the mills press the AOP olive oil of the Baux valley, which tastes of the place and keeps for a long time.

Photo: Jacques Le Letty, CC BY-SA 4.0

At Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, it is Glanum that will hold you, an ancient city set at the mouth of a valley, where the Romans came after the Greeks and where the streets can still be read in the ground. Right next door, behind a wall, there is a former monastery where Van Gogh lived for a year.

Wednesday morning is when you can walk the market that spreads along the boulevards ringing the village. You will find Provençal produce, olive oil, tapenade, pastis, local honey, and also Provençal fabrics, tablecloths, place mats and aprons printed with tiny motifs, olives, cicadas, sprigs, in yellows and blues that have not dated in two centuries. Those motifs arrived from India through the port of Marseille three centuries ago. The king banned them to protect the silk weavers of Lyon. Provence went on making them anyway. So there is a little of India under the southern French sun.

On to Arles. Its arena and its underground galleries recall the Roman era, and they are remarkably well preserved. Here, the bull is king. Two traditions share the arena: the Spanish corrida, which comes from the other side of the Pyrenees and in which the animal dies, and the course camarguaise, which belongs here.

That is the one to see. Men dressed all in white have to snatch a red rosette fixed between the horns of the star. The bull waits for them, watches them, picks his target and goes. Half a tonne of animal charging straight at you does not brake: the raseteur cuts across in front of the horns, hand outstretched towards the forehead, and runs for the barrier, which he clears with both feet as the bull slams into it behind him. The best of them take the rosette a few centimetres from the horn. Raseteurs are sometimes injured, that is the price of glory. The bull, for his part, always goes home safe to his field, and some of them even get a statue.

Van Gogh spent fifteen months in Arles, from February 1888 to May 1889, and painted more than two hundred canvases there. Yet, oddly, you will find very little of him. Not one of his paintings can be seen in Arles. The Yellow House where he lived was destroyed by a bombing raid in 1944. So was the bridge he painted.

What remains is the Alyscamps, that avenue of Roman sarcophagi where he set up his easel beside Gauguin, and which has not changed. What remains is the courtyard of the old hospital where he was treated after the business with the ear, replanted exactly as in his painting. And what remains is a foundation bearing his name, opened in 2014, which borrows paintings in order to show them: the town has to rent its own painter.

The rest has to be said. In his lifetime, the people of Arles signed a petition to have him locked up. They called him the redheaded madman. He left for Saint-Rémy in May 1889. It is in Saint-Rémy that you will find out far more about the painter:

In summer, the Rencontres d'Arles bring photography into every corner of the town, right down to deconsecrated chapels.

From Arles, the road drops towards the delta. The Camargue begins without warning: the trees lie down, the water takes over everything, marshes, lagoons, rice fields. The white horses live outdoors, half wild. So do the bulls. As for the flamingos, they sometimes stay so far off that you will take them for a rumour.

At Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, the sea returns all at once, and every year gypsies from all over Europe come on pilgrimage to carry Sara down to the waves. At table, it will be gardiane de taureau, bull stew, local rice and fleur de sel from the salt pans.

05

What to eat in the Bouches-du-Rhône

At table, everything starts with pastis. It is drunk in the shade at the end of the morning, with cracked olives from the Baux valley, a tapenade, that paste of crushed black olives with capers and anchovies, and an anchoïade, an anchovy dip for bread and raw vegetables. Warm panisses, those fried chickpea-flour sticks, round off the aperitif.

Around Marseille, do not miss a bouillabaisse at Fonfon, in the vallon des Auffes. Nobody can say it is the best, but it is the best known, and the setting alone is worth the fish: a tiny cove wedged under the bridge, fishermen's huts, boats, and the sea lapping three metres from your table.

The dish is served in two stages, and that is how you tell the real thing. First the broth, thick, saffron-scented, drawn from rock fish caught that morning. Into it you dip croutons rubbed with garlic and spread with rouille, that paste of garlic, chilli, egg yolk and olive oil which wakes your palate up in one go. Only then do the fish arrive, whole, carved in front of you. Scorpionfish, John Dory, weever, conger: the owner names them one by one, and he is right to. Know what you are getting into: the real thing starts at 70 euros for the dish, reckon on around a hundred euros a head once the wine and the rest are in, and it has to be ordered ahead, often the day before, and never for one person alone. It is a meal, not a dish. Anyone who produces one in ten minutes on the Vieux-Port is selling you something else.

Other dishes await, and they do not go in for lightness. Pieds et paquets were born out of the offal nobody wanted: mutton tripe rolled into little parcels and tied by hand, lamb's trotters, and hours of simmering in white wine. It was Sunday food in working-class homes; today it is served in good restaurants. Friday is for the grand aïoli: salt cod soaked out, boiled vegetables, and in the middle a garlic sauce whipped up with olive oil that will see you through the day. Nobody works well the afternoon that follows. And then there are the petits farcis, which every grandmother here makes on Sundays: tomatoes, courgettes, onions and aubergines hollowed out, filled with minced meat and put in the oven. They are eaten warm, never hot, and they are better the next day.

At Martigues, they salt and press mullet roe, dry it, and that gives poutargue. The locals call it their caviar and they do not joke about it. The fish comes from the lagoon, right there. It is eaten in thin slices with a trickle of oil and a glass of white wine, and it is worth its price by the gram.

Past Arles, you no longer eat the same things. Rice colonised the delta because the land had to be flushed of its salt, and it never left: it is rice they serve you under the gardiane, that bull stew which soaks overnight in red wine before simmering a whole afternoon with black olives. The meat comes from the farms next door, the ones whose animals you saw that morning, running free in the marshes. As for the salt, the salt workers still gather it by hand from the salt pans, and it is that fleur de sel that will end up on your tomato.

On the beaches, look out for tellines. They are clams the size of a fingernail, which fishermen scrape from the wet sand with a frame on the end of a pole, walking backwards in twenty centimetres of water. They are served to you sautéed with garlic and parsley, in a deep dish. You empty them one by one, you mop up the juice, and you lose track of the time.

As for wine, Cassis makes white on a tiny vineyard wedged between the cliff and the sea, and that white was designed for rock fish: order it with the bouillabaisse and you will see why it never leaves the region. The Coteaux d'Aix cover everything else, rosé you drink cold without asking questions. And if you are eating lamb, ask for a red from Les Baux: the valley has gone almost entirely organic, and you can taste it.

That leaves the sweet things. An oven near the abbey of Saint-Victor has been baking navettes since 1781, those hard orange-blossom biscuits shaped like a little boat, recalling the boat of the Saintes of the Camargue. They keep for weeks and they are made for dunking in coffee. In Aix, calissons have been made since the fifteenth century, almond and candied melon on a wafer, and the archbishop still blesses a batch every September. And at Christmas, the pompe à l'huile, an olive-oil brioche, arrives on the table of the thirteen desserts: that one you must break by hand. Cutting it with a knife brings bad luck, and nobody at the table will let you do it.

06

The local sports

Two things will follow you everywhere. Pétanque was born here, in La Ciotat, in 1907: a player could no longer run his three-step approach, so he played with his feet planted, pieds tanqués in Provençal. The name stuck. A game is played to 13 points, and anyone who fails to score is fanny: he will go and kiss the backside of the Fanny, that wooden or ceramic plaque which every self-respecting club keeps hanging behind the bar. Every summer, the parc Borély hosts the Mondial la Marseillaise, the biggest competition in the world, and anyone can enter.

And then there is OM. You will not need to look for football, it will find you: in bar conversations, on the walls, in the car horns on the night of a win. The Vélodrome can be visited. But try being there on match night, and all the more so, if you are feeling brave, when the age-old enemy comes to town, Paris Saint-Germain. That night, the whole city talks about nothing else.

07

How long to stay

Marseille can be seen in two days at a run, three at a gentler pace, and four or five if you want to make the most of it. Cassis and Le Castellet in one busy day. Aubagne is mainly worth it in December, or if you do the hills and the play. Aix-en-Provence, allow a day. The Côte Bleue can be done in two or three days. The Alpilles the same. And the Camargue in two days.

But those are minimums. Provence is not seen at a run, it is discovered slowly. You will want to enjoy the beaches, the afternoon nap, the drinks in village bars under the plane trees. So our advice is to take your time and enjoy it, forget our minimums and live the Provençal way, without undue stress. Except in traffic jams, or on match nights.

08

When to go to the Bouches-du-Rhône

April, May, June. Or September and October. The sun is the same, the sea is still good in September, and you will have the place to yourself.

July and August are another matter. The heat crushes you, the villages overflow, and above all the Calanques massif closes: the prefecture decides each evening, for the next day, according to the fire risk and the strength of the wind. You may well wake up one August morning to learn that your walk is off. In season, access is capped in any case, and you have to book your place in advance for some calanques. The Côte Bleue roads also close to cars on summer weekends.

There are also dates worth the trip on their own. In February, Carry-le-Rouet puts sea urchins out on the harbour. In May, the gypsies come down to Saintes-Maries. In early summer, the Cocarde d'Or is run in the Arles arena, and the photography festival takes over the whole town until September. In December, santons invade Aubagne and Marseille. Each of them fills the local hotels months ahead: ask before you book, not after.

And then we have to talk about the mistral. It is the north wind, it comes down the Rhône valley and it arrives without warning. It blows in gusts, it slams the shutters, it flattens the cypresses that farmers planted as windbreaks, it cancels your boat trip and it makes the Digue à la Mer unrideable. They say it blows for three days, six days or nine. It is not true, but everyone repeats it.

It has one merit all the same, and it is an enormous one: it is what makes the light. After it has passed, the sky is washed clean, the air turns transparent, the Alpilles are cut out with a knife and Mont Ventoux appears a hundred kilometres away. That light Van Gogh and Cézanne came looking for is given to them by the mistral. You will curse it in the morning. You will thank it in the evening.

09

Getting there and around

You will arrive at Marseille-Provence airport, at Marignane, set on the edge of the Étang de Berre. It is the second-largest airport outside Paris after Nice, it serves the whole of Europe, and it puts you twenty-five kilometres from the Vieux-Port. A shuttle links the terminal to Saint-Charles station in half an hour. Note that Avignon, Nîmes and Toulon also have airports: depending on where you are coming from, one of them may suit you better.

But the best way to arrive here is the train. The TGV puts Marseille three hours from Paris and drops you in the very centre, at Saint-Charles, at the top of a monumental staircase that throws the city in your face. Aix-en-Provence has its own TGV station, out in open country fifteen kilometres from the town, and Avignon has one just as far out. Marseille remains the most convenient way in: it is the only TGV station in the department that is genuinely in a city.

After that, the regional trains do the work. From Saint-Charles they run to Aix, to Aubagne, to Cassis and La Ciotat, to Arles and Avignon, and above all to Miramas along the Côte Bleue, a line that runs on viaducts above the inlets and is worth the ticket on its own. One caveat: the coastal stations are often perched a long way from the harbours, and the one at Cassis is a good three kilometres from the village, at the top of a hill. Check the shuttle before setting off on foot with your luggage.

Coaches fill in the gaps, under the regional network. The Marseille to Aix line runs all day and much of the night, it is a route apart. The rest serves the villages on a school timetable: excellent Monday to Friday, far less so at weekends and in summer. Check today's timetable, not the one you think you know.

Within Marseille, two metro lines and three tram lines cover the centre, with buses and an old ferry boat that crosses the Vieux-Port in five minutes for next to nothing. Aix is walked, entirely. So is Arles.

Should you hire a car? For the first part of your trip, no. Marseille, Aix, Cassis, the Côte Bleue, L'Estaque, Arles: all of it can be done by train and bus, and you will spare yourself the hunt for a parking space. In summer, on the Côte Bleue, a car is actually a handicap: the roads to the inlets close at weekends and the pound takes care of the rest.

For the second part, yes. The Alpilles cannot be visited any other way: the villages are scattered, the school coaches will not take you to Les Baux-de-Provence on a Sunday in April, and Glanum has no station. The Camargue is worse still: the distances are long, flat and empty, and one coach line links Arles to Saintes-Maries without serving much in between. Take the train to Arles, hire the car there, drop it off on the way back. You will get the best of both.

10

Off the beaten track: what the guidebooks forget

Shall we go to hell?

Head north of Les Baux-de-Provence, into the Val d'Enfer, the Valley of Hell. The road enters between two white limestone cliffs that erosion has carved into needles and silhouettes. But it is the holes that strike you: everywhere in the rock, dozens of black openings, square galleries, cavities the eye cannot see the end of. Men dug all this for centuries to get the stone out. They left; the holes stayed.

You can see why legend put the witches' sabbath here. Cocteau understood before anyone: he filmed Testament of Orpheus here in 1959. And in the largest of those galleries, Van Gogh is now projected onto the walls.

Want more of the strange? On the edge of Saint-Rémy there is a working psychiatric hospital, Saint-Paul-de-Mausole. Franciscans opened an asylum here in 1605, the Revolution closed it, a doctor reopened it in 1807, and treatment has never stopped since. Today it is a clinic for women: while you are visiting, doctors will be working and patients will be receiving care behind the walls you walk along.

Because you can visit. Van Gogh went in of his own accord on 8 May 1889, after the business with the ear. He came out a year later. In between, he painted like a man possessed, Irises, The Starry Night, the wheat and the cypresses he could see from his bedroom window, the very room you will walk into. The Romanesque cloister has been there since the Middle Ages. And the paintings hung along the way were painted here, in the art therapy studio, by patients.

At Tarascon, the castle is one of the best preserved in France, and it owes that to its past as a prison: men were locked up here for four hundred and forty-five years, until 1926. The prisoners' graffiti is still there, carved into the stone.

Between the Alpilles and the sea lies the Crau, and nobody stops there. It is a desert of pebbles, the former delta of the Durance, and the only steppe in France. Sheep have grazed it since the Neolithic. Birds live there that are seen nowhere else in France.

And at the entrance to Saint-Chamas, beside a roundabout, a Roman bridge still crosses the Touloubre. The Pont Flavien carries a triumphal arch at each end, one on either side of the crossing, and it is the only Roman bridge in France to have kept them both. Carts rolled over it for so long that the stone still holds the ruts.

Speaking the local language

To finish, we shall teach you to speak properly so you can make yourself understood. A few local words you will hear often:

Barjaquer
To barjaque is to chat away.
Cacou
The one showing off to get himself noticed (stop being such a cacou!).
Cagade
When you do something stupid, that is a cagade.
Cagnard
The blazing heat under the sun. Qué cagnard! On the Vieux-Port, in summer, there is not a single tree.
Cagole
A girl with a bit of a reputation, deliberate or otherwise 😊.
Va caguer!
How to put this politely: go and sit on the toilet!
Dégun
Y a dégun ici! Very widely used. There is nobody here.
Escagassé
Worn out.
Fada
The fada is the madman.
Fatche!
Or fatche de con!, the local exclamation par excellence. It works for surprise, admiration and annoyance.
Jobastre
Qué jobastre: what a madman, he takes too many risks.
Méfi
Watch out!
Minot
Child, kid.
Morfale
The morfale eats everything, he is a glutton.
Pastisser
Nothing to do with pastis. It means to get yourself dirty.
Péguer
It sticks (spill coke on a table and once it dries, ça pègue).
Peuchère
The word for pity. You say it for someone out of luck (peuchère, he missed his train).
Pitchoun
The little one, the kid. It is also said to an adult you are fond of.
Prendre une rouste
To take a hiding.
Scoumoune
To have the scoumoune is to be jinxed.
Tarpin
A lot, very. C'est tarpin bien. Also very widely used.
