01

Van, panel van, motorhome: what we mean

The word "van" doesn't mean the same thing in Reykjavik, Los Angeles or Windhoek. Before comparing prices, you have to compare the vehicles.

Camper vehicles generally fall into three families. The compact van is the small format, like the Volkswagen California: two seats, a pop-up roof, a minimal kitchen. Easy to drive, easy to park, perfect for a couple. The panel van (camper van) is bigger: you can stand up inside, and it often has a real toilet and shower. The motorhome (Americans say "RV", New Zealanders "motorhome") is the biggest: a house on wheels, four to seven berths, but a beast to manoeuvre and park.

This distinction isn't just detail. It decides three concrete things: the licence you need, the price you'll pay, and where you can spend the night. A compact van parks in town, a motorhome doesn't. Keep these three formats in mind for everything that follows.

02

Where you can really rent a van

Van life is everywhere on Instagram, but organised rental is concentrated in a handful of destinations where the infrastructure keeps up: serious rental companies, service areas, campsites, suitable roads. There's no point dreaming of a brand-new panel van in a country that doesn't rent them.

The big van rental markets today are Western Europe (France, Spain, Portugal, Germany), Iceland, the United States and Canada, New Zealand, Australia, and Southern Africa (Namibia, South Africa, Botswana). These are the areas where you'll find fleets, choice and roadside assistance if something goes wrong.

Each has its own logic. Iceland and New Zealand thrive on vans because it's the smartest way to travel there. The United States runs on big RVs. Southern Africa rents 4x4s with roof tents, not panel vans. We'll break all this down below, but keep in mind that "renting a van" doesn't play out the same way from one continent to the next.

03

The licence, the tricky bit

This is the first thing to sort out, even before comparing prices. The simple rule: as long as the vehicle weighs under 3.5 tonnes, a standard car licence is enough. That covers almost all compact vans and panel vans. You get in and you drive.

The problem comes with the big ones. Above 3.5 tonnes, you need a heavier licence category (in Europe, a C1). That mainly applies to large integrated motorhomes and some American RVs. In the United States, most rental motorhomes can still be driven on a standard licence, but always check the exact weight of the model before signing, because the biggest ones tip over the limit.

Two other conditions come up everywhere, regardless of the licence. The minimum age: often 21, sometimes 25 with premium companies or in Iceland, with a surcharge for young drivers. And the length of time you've held your licence: most companies require at least one year, sometimes two.

One last detail that catches out travellers from other continents: the international driving permit. To drive in the United States, New Zealand, Australia or Namibia with a foreign licence, you're often asked for an official translation or an international driving permit alongside your national one. It's usually obtained before departure, but you need to plan ahead: check the rules of the country you're visiting and those of your home country.

04

The 7 things to check before you pay

The price shown on the homepage means almost nothing. The real cost, and the real nasty surprises, hide in the terms and conditions. Here's what actually matters, in order.

  • Insurance and the excess. Basic insurance is almost always included, but the excess (what you pay out of pocket if there's damage) can run to several thousand. Buying down the excess is optional and expensive, but it's often what turns a small scrape into a nightmare or a non-event.
  • The deposit. An amount held on your card for the whole trip, sometimes very high. Check the amount and how it's held: your card limit needs to cover it.
  • Mileage. Unlimited or capped changes everything on a road trip. A capped package that looks cheap becomes ruinous the moment you go over the quota. On long distances (United States, Australia), insist on unlimited.
  • One-way drop-off. Picking up the van in one place and returning it in another is very handy, but the relocation fees can be steep, often several hundred. Sometimes companies are instead looking for someone to move a van, and then it's almost free.
  • The season. Prices can double in high season. In Iceland, a summer van costs twice its winter rate. Shifting by a month can change the bill by 40%.
  • Included equipment. Bedding, kitchenware, chairs, table, heating, GPS: sometimes included, sometimes charged extra. Make the list beforehand, or the bill swells at the counter.
  • The extra driver. Adding a second driver usually costs a small daily fee. On a long trip, sharing the wheel is quickly worth it.

One simple way to sort the wheat from the chaff: ask for an itemised quote, line by line. A company that refuses to put all of this in writing before you book is a company to avoid.

05

Professional rental or peer-to-peer

There are two broad ways to rent, with two opposing logics. On one side, the professional rental company: a business with its own fleet, its branches, its 24/7 assistance. Consistent vehicles, a reassuring setup, slightly higher prices. It's the default choice for a first van trip, when you want to sleep easy.

On the other, peer-to-peer rental: platforms that connect van owners with renters, like an Airbnb for vehicles. In the United States, Outdoorsy and RVshare dominate; in France, players like Yescapa. You'll find an enormous variety of vehicles, often lower prices, but also more variability: the state of the van and how professional it feels depend on the owner.

Between the two, aggregators compare offers from several professional companies in a single search: CamperDays, Motorhome Republic, Camper Champ, Indie Campers and others. Handy for narrowing things down and comparing, but always read the final company's terms, not just the aggregator's headline price.

06

A country-by-country tour of rentals

Here are the great van destinations, each with its own logic and its reference players. The names below are given as a guide: fleets, prices and terms change, so always check when you book.

New Zealand · The best-equipped country
New Zealand

Few countries are as well organised for vans. The market is highly concentrated: the Tourism Holdings group (THL), the world's largest RV rental group, owns three brands that are really three price tiers of the same fleet, Maui, Britz and Mighty. JUCY is its main challenger, and Apollo covers one-way trips between the North and South Islands well.

Reference playersTHL (Maui, Britz, Mighty), JUCY, Apollo; many independents known for their service
Good to knowThe biggest companies aren't always the best rated
Key pointA "self-contained" vehicle unlocks extra overnight spots
One-wayCommon between Auckland, Christchurch and Queenstown

Iceland · The van paradise
Iceland

Nowhere is the case for a van stronger than here. More than 170 campsites line the Ring Road, the most spectacular sites are just minutes from Route 1, and a van combines transport and accommodation in a country where hotels are expensive. Almost all companies hand over the vehicles at Keflavík, near the airport.

Reference playersGo Campers, Touring Cars, Hertz Iceland; aggregators Motorhome Republic, Camper Champ
Good to knowMinimum age often 25, licence held for at least one year
BudgetHighly seasonal; summer can cost double the winter rate
Key pointWild camping is strictly banned, campsites only

United States · The kingdom of the RV
United States and Canada

Here it's mostly about big motorhomes (RVs). Two worlds coexist: fleet operators, led by Cruise America (over 130 branches), reassuring and standardised; and peer-to-peer platforms, Outdoorsy and RVshare, offering huge choice at variable prices. Escape Campervans offers more compact vans for those who don't want a behemoth.

Australia · The wide open spaces
Australia

Same market logic as New Zealand, and often the same groups. Britz and Apollo belong to the thl group and run recent fleets; JUCY and Travellers Autobarn target tight budgets and backpackers. Most vehicles can be driven on a standard licence and offer unlimited mileage, which is precious given the distances.

Reference playersBritz, Apollo (thl group), JUCY, Travellers Autobarn
Good to knowSome companies accept under-21s, others don't
One-wayPossible but pricey between major cities; sometimes cheap relocation deals

Southern Africa · The 4x4 with a roof tent
Namibia, South Africa, Botswana

Here, forget the panel van. The king of the road is the 4x4 fitted with a roof tent and all the camping gear built in, designed for self-sufficient safaris and dirt tracks. You sleep up high, safe from the wildlife, and you cross borders between countries (Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe) in the same vehicle.

Reference playersBritz 4x4, Bushlore, Bobo Campers

LicenceThe biggest vehicles (9-seat safari) may require a heavier licence category and an approved driver

In Western Europe, van rental is well established: Spain, Portugal, Germany and Italy have active markets, served by franchise networks and by the big peer-to-peer platforms. The logic everywhere is the same: a professional network for peace of mind, peer-to-peer for choice and price.

France · The franchise networks
France

The French market is built around a few franchise networks that often fit out their own vehicles. The oldest is VAN-IT, launched in 2008 by Vivien Mazeyrie, at a time when rental vans barely existed in France and only motorhomes were represented. Starting from a handful of vehicles south of Paris, the network is now present in around thirty towns, with a few outposts in Spain and Portugal. Its direct competitors, Blacksheep Van and WeVan, also cover the country through franchising.

Reference playersVAN-IT (pioneer, 2008), Blacksheep Van, WeVan; peer-to-peer platforms such as Yescapa
Good to knowPick-up at a branch, near your starting point, which avoids long transfers
VehiclesOften Volkswagen Californias and panel vans, in 2- and 5-berth ranges
Key pointA professional network for peace of mind, peer-to-peer for choice and price

07

Where you can legally sleep

This is the question that ruins poorly prepared trips. The right to spend the night somewhere varies enormously from one country to the next, and a fine or a knock on the window from the police quickly kills the dream of freedom.

At the extremes: Scotland allows wild camping almost anywhere under its right of responsible access. Iceland, by contrast, strictly bans wild camping: you sleep only at campsites, and there are thankfully plenty of them. New Zealand distinguishes "self-contained" vehicles (with a built-in toilet), which can park at far more sites than others.

Elsewhere the rule is often nuanced: in many countries, parking a van on the public road is tolerated (you're only parking), but "camping" in the strict sense, table and awning out, is banned outside designated areas. In France as on many coastlines, councils ban it explicitly in summer. The good practice everywhere: arrive late, leave early, leave no trace, and favour official service areas and the apps that list authorised spots.
