Yorkshire's Most Beautiful Places : 5 stops

York

Suggested base : York
★★★★☆
1

Start with The Shambles, England's most photographed medieval street. The 14th-century timber-framed houses lean so close to one another they nearly touch overhead, forming a dim corridor you might recognize: the street inspired Diagon Alley in Harry Potter, and several shops fully embrace the theme. The street was once occupied by butchers—iron hooks for hanging meat are still visible on some façades. Arrive before 9am to see it without the tour coaches.

60 min
2

York Minster is the largest medieval cathedral in England—160 meters long, with a 72-meter central tower, built over 250 years between 1220 and 1472. Two facts capture the scale: the Great East Window measures 23 meters high by 9.8 wide, roughly the size of a tennis court, and it is the largest expanse of medieval stained glass in the world. The cathedral holds more original medieval glass than all other English cathedrals combined. In 1984, lightning triggered a fire that destroyed part of the roof—the 15th-century Rose Window in the south transept survived. Allow at least 90 minutes, and climb the 275 steps up the central tower for the view over York.

120 min
3

Walking around York's city walls is one of the best ways to put a place's history in context in England. The medieval walls run for about 3.2 kilometers and rank among the best-preserved in the country—some sections rest on Roman foundations dating to AD 70. Allow 1 hour 30 minutes for the full loop, or walk the stretch between Bootham Bar and Monk Bar for views of the Minster.

90 min
4

Clifford's Tower is York's darkest symbol. This 13th-century limestone keep, perched on its artificial motte, marks the site of an 1190 massacre in which hundreds of members of York's Jewish community died during an antisemitic riot. A memorial plaque has been installed at the tower's base. The view from the top over the city rooftops is worth the climb—and the brutal history is told honestly in the exhibition inside.

45 min
5

The National Railway Museum, free and a short walk from the station, is the world's largest railway museum. Among its collection sits Mallard, the locomotive that claimed the world speed record for steam in 1938 at 202.8 km/h—a record that still stands. Allow at least 2 hours, easily 3 with children.

120 min

Activities

Stop at the Guy Fawkes Inn. Guy Fawkes, the protagonist of the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, was born in this house in 1570 - the plaque confirms it. The pub is still here, and a pint in the vaulted ground-floor room remains one of the most authentic experiences in central York.

45 min

At the end of the day, walk along King's Staith, the quay beside the River Ouse in the heart of York. Watch the boats from the riverbank and look at the waterfront façades. Continue toward Lendal Bridge to discover central York from the quays and photograph the riverside scenery.

60 min

York is an excellent city for dining out. The lanes around The Shambles are full of good places to eat, and Shambles Market offers local street food stalls. Choose Yorkshire cheeses or, for purists, a baked Yorkshire pudding.

Thornton Le Dale

Suggested base : Whitby · 29 km
★★☆☆☆
1

Plan a day trip from Thornton-le-Dale to visit Knaresborough. Knaresborough sets the tone on arrival: limestone houses cling to a cliff above the River Nidd, and the Victorian viaduct cuts across the horizon like a film backdrop. Walk down to the water for the classic view of the boats and colorful façades—this is where the town really makes sense, rather than from above.

45 min
2

Plan a day trip from Thornton-le-Dale to visit Knaresborough Castle. Knaresborough Castle has overlooked the Nidd since the 14th century. It was built by the Normans and dismantled after the English Civil War on Cromwell's orders in 1648; only the square keep remains—but the view from the gardens overlooking the cliff is remarkable. Allow around 45 minutes for the on-site museum, which traces the town's history.

60 min
3

Plan a day trip from Thornton-le-Dale to visit Mother Shipton's Cave. Do not miss Mother Shipton's Cave, officially England's oldest visitor attraction with an admission charge, open to the public since 1630. The site consists of two wonders side by side on the banks of the Nidd: the cave where the prophetess Ursula Southeil is said to have been born in 1488 (she supposedly predicted the Spanish Armada and the Great Fire of London), and the Petrifying Well, whose mineral-rich water gradually encrusts objects hung beneath it—a teddy bear takes three to five months to “petrify”. Allow at least an hour and buy your ticket online, especially in high season.

75 min
4

Plan a day trip from Thornton-le-Dale to the White Horse of Kilburn and Sutton Bank. On your way north, stop at the White Horse of Kilburn for one of Yorkshire's most unobstructed views. This horse figure, 96 meters long and 70 meters high, was cut into the chalk on the hillside in 1857—it is visible from over 30 kilometers away in clear weather. The nearby Sutton Bank viewpoint offers a panorama over the whole plain of York that is worth the detour on its own.

45 min
5

Thornton-le-Dale rounds off the day in fitting style. The village has been regularly voted Yorkshire's most beautiful—since at least 1907, when a newspaper poll bestowed that title. Walk around the village green with its 13th-century medieval cross and stocks (last documented use: 1874), then follow the stream to Beck Isle Cottage, a Grade II listed 17th-century timber-framed thatched cottage that appears on thousands of calendars and chocolate boxes. The house is private—it can be admired from outside, from the small stone bridge.

60 min
6

Before leaving the village, walk to All Saints Church in the eastern part of the village. The building dates to the 14th century, but evidence of religious use of the site goes back to the 7th century. Inside, look for musket ball marks on the nave pillars—they date from the English Civil War, when a group of Parliamentarian soldiers sought refuge here from Royalists.

30 min

Activities

An evening walk along Thornton Beck, the stream running through the village. The 2-mile route to the hamlet of Ellerburn and its St Hilda's Church (dating back to at least 1050) is peaceful and easy—allow about an hour. It is best to set off just before the light begins to fade.

60 min

The village has several independent cafés and pubs around the green, ideal for a late-day stop. If you pass through in August, the Thornton-le-Dale Scarecrow Festival transforms the village into a life-size scarecrow gallery—a strangely endearing event.

45 min

Whitby

Suggested base : Whitby
★★★★☆
1

Plan a day trip from Whitby to visit Dalby Forest. From Thornton-le-Dale, the road to the coast follows the southern fringe of the North York Moors and passes through Dalby Forest—3,200 hectares of woodland managed by Forestry England. The visitor center is a good starting point for a short hour-long walk on the marked trails. Roe deer are common early in the morning. If you are traveling with children, the marked mountain bike trails are excellent. Then take the A171 east—the forest gradually gives way to open moorland, followed by the first sea views: arriving in Whitby along the clifftop, with the abbey in silhouette, is one of England's most beautiful approaches to a town.

75 min
2

Whitby Abbey is the main reason to make the detour to this coast. Founded in 657 by Abbess Hild on orders from King Oswiu of Northumbria, the Benedictine abbey was dissolved in 1539 under Henry VIII—the Gothic ruins you see today date mainly from the 13th century. In 1890, Bram Stoker spent his vacation in Whitby, sat on a bench in St Mary's churchyard observing the ruins, noted the name “Dracula” he'd just found in a book at the local library, and invented his novel's opening scene—the ship Demeter running aground in the harbor, and the Count racing up the 199 steps in the form of a large black dog. Climb those same steps in the evening to see the ruins in silhouette: it's exactly the scene he saw.

90 min
3

St Mary's Church, right beside the abbey, is one of England's most unusual churches: the 18th-century interior is a labyrinth of wooden galleries on multiple levels, pews kept locked by the families that own them, and painted balustrades. In the graveyard, look for the tombstones Stoker copied to name his characters, including that of “Swales”.

40 min
4

Whitby is divided in two by the River Esk: the old town on the east side with its fishermen's alleys, its jet jewelry (made from local black stone fashionable in Victorian times), and its herring smokehouses; and the more commercial part of town on the west. Walk along the east quay and the piers to see the boats. The harbor remains active—trawlers land catches each morning.

60 min
5

Robin Hood's Bay is well worth the detour from Whitby - on the coast south of the town. This 18th-century smugglers' village is packed so tightly against the cliff that only one road leads down to the sea: everything else is a succession of cobbled lanes where two people can barely pass. At low tide, the beach reveals fossils in the rock - ammonites and belemnites in abundance. The Cleveland Way, one of England's most renowned coastal trails, passes through here.

75 min

Activities

Fish and chips is an institution in Whitby, and several harbor establishments have competed for the title of Yorkshire's best for generations. Grab a table facing the port—the view of old Whitby with the abbey backdrop makes it one of the country's most satisfying lunches.

60 min

A night-time walk around the 199 steps and the cemetery remains a distinctive experience.

90 min

Haworth

Suggested base : Haworth
★★☆☆☆
1

In Haworth, walk up cobbled Main Street toward the Brontë Parsonage Museum. As you climb, you can see stone façades and alleyways branching off the main street. Stop at the windows of the independent shops, then continue to the area around the parsonage and St Michael and All Angels Church. The churchyard gives a glimpse of the Brontë family's everyday surroundings. This walk takes you through the village on foot, before visiting the museum or joining the moorland trails.

75 min
2

From Haworth, walk to Penistone Hill to discover the moorland near the village. The paths cross open ground with grasses, heather, and rocky outcrops. Take in the views of the hills and valley, then return toward the village along the marked trails. The landscape helps you understand the setting of the Brontë sisters' novels without undertaking the hike to Top Withens. Bring shoes suitable for wet paths and a jacket to protect against the wind.

75 min
3

Haworth is one of Europe's most visited literary pilgrimage destinations. Main Street climbs steeply toward the Brontë parsonage—as steep as in 1820 when the family first climbed it on arrival. The Brontë Parsonage Museum is the house where Patrick Brontë settled as parish priest in 1820, where Charlotte, Emily, and Anne grew up, and where the three sisters wrote Jane Eyre, Wuthering Heights, and The Tenant of Wildfell Hall between 1840 and 1848. The museum holds the world's largest collection of Brontë manuscripts and objects: the microscopic handwriting of the “Little Books” (childhood notebooks), Charlotte's sewing box, Emily's painting easel. Allow between 1 hour 30 minutes and 2 hours.

120 min
4

Across the street, St Michael and All Angels Church is where Patrick Brontë ministered—and where Charlotte, Emily, Branwell, and their parents are buried. The current building dates from 1879 (the original, considered dilapidated, was demolished), but the atmosphere of the cemetery surrounding the parsonage—with its leaning gravestones visible from the windows of the dining room where the sisters wrote—remains striking.

30 min
5

Behind the parsonage, the moors begin literally meters away. The path to Top Withens, the ruined farm thought to have inspired Wuthering Heights, starts from the end of the garden and climbs to a windswept plateau—6 kilometers round trip; allow 2 hours 30 minutes. Even if you don't do the full hike, walk for 20 minutes onto the moor to feel the wind that shaped the sisters' imagination.

60 min

Activities

The Keighley & Worth Valley Railway steam line connects Keighley to Oxenhope via Haworth—8 kilometers on a heritage line entirely run by volunteers since 1968. The half-hour journey is a fine way to arrive in or leave Haworth if you plan around the timetable (check the website before departing). The line served as the setting for the 1970 film “The Railway Children”.

60 min

Haworth makes it easy to spend an evening at the pub: Main Street and its side lanes have several establishments in dark 19th-century sandstone buildings. The village also hosts an annual 1940s festival (dates vary) that transforms the village into a wartime setting with uniforms, period vehicles, and swing concerts.

60 min

Malham

Suggested base : Haworth · 29 km
★★★★☆
1

Plan a day trip from Malham to visit Ribblehead. The road from Haworth crosses the Dales via the north—allow time for the approach. Stop at Ribblehead for the viaduct that has carried the Settle–Carlisle line above the moor since 1874. The figures are staggering: 24 arches, 404 meters long, 32 meters above the ground, 1.5 million bricks, and over 100 workers who died during the five years of construction—the Midland Railway funded the expansion of the local cemetery to bury them. Every sixth pier is 50% thicker than the others, designed to keep the structure standing if a pier collapsed. In 1989, British Rail nearly closed the line before a citizens' campaign saved it. Passenger trains still run along it today—if you see one passing, the sight is striking.

45 min
2

Janet's Foss is the waterfall to visit before Gordale Scar—a cascade tucked in the woods at the end of a 20-minute walk from Malham. Local legend associates the cave behind the falls with Janet, queen of Yorkshire's fairies. In summer, people bathe in the natural pool.

45 min
3

Gordale Scar is one of England's most impressive gorges—a 100-meter-high fissure in the limestone, formed by glacial meltwater, which you enter through a narrow opening before discovering a waterfall inside. Turner painted it in 1808. Wordsworth wrote an ode. In dry periods, a moderately challenging scramble alongside the waterfall lets you emerge at the top. Bring shoes with good grip.

60 min
4

Malham Cove is the highlight of the trip through the Dales. This amphitheater-shaped limestone cliff measures 80 meters high and 300 meters wide—it was formed about 12,000 years ago by a gigantic glacial waterfall whose water vanished underground in the early 19th century. Climb the 400 steps on the left side to reach the top, where you'll find a limestone pavement—a plateau of limestone blocks separated by deep fissures (the “grykes”) that give it a lunar appearance. This landscape served as a setting in Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows – Part 1. Peregrine falcons have nested in the cliff since 1993—telescopes are installed below to observe them.

90 min
5

Malham Tarn, 2 kilometers north of the Cove, is a geological anomaly in this limestone landscape: the mountain lake formed on an impermeable Silurian slate layer—the only one in the area—that prevents water from seeping away. It's one of Europe's rare high-altitude alkaline lakes and a national nature reserve. A short walk from the road lets you walk partway around it.

45 min
6

For a gentle end to the day, head down toward Settle and the cliffs of Attermire Scar. This long limestone spur above town contains several prehistoric caves including Victoria Cave, excavated in the 19th century—archaeologists found bones of hippopotamuses, rhinoceroses, and hyenas dating to an interglacial period about 120,000 years ago, when Yorkshire had a subtropical climate. The walk from Settle takes 45 minutes one way.

60 min

Activities

The scenic road between Ribblehead, Settle, and Malham is one of northern England's finest—limestone ridges, dry-stone walls, isolated farms, deep valleys. If weather permits, take the B6479 from Ribblehead toward Horton-in-Ribblesdale then the B6265 to Malham for the best scenic route.

60 min

The Yorkshire Dales are designated a Dark Sky Reserve—one of England's best-protected areas with low light pollution. At night in Malham, far from cities, the Milky Way is visible to the naked eye in clear weather. Stay out half an hour after sunset to watch the sky open up.

60 min
