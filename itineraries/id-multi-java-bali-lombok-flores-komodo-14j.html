Yogyakarta

🛏️ Suggested base : Yogyakarta
★★★★★
1

Arrival in Yogyakarta and a first plunge into the atmosphere of Java's cultural capital. Head straight for Jalan Malioboro, the city's main artery — one kilometer of wide sidewalks lined with batik shops, street warungs and hawkers. Here becaks (pedal rickshaws) and horse-drawn carriages weave through the pedestrians. It's the best introduction to the Javanese rhythm: slow, warm, threaded with gamelan music drifting from the shopfronts.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Kraton of Yogyakarta—the sultan's palace—is the historical and spiritual heart of the city. Construction began in 1755 under Hamengkubuwono I and continued for forty years. It's a city within a city: behind its enclosing walls, one kilometer on each side, lie marble courtyards, teak pavilions, mosques and residences still occupied today. Yogyakarta is one of the only regions in Indonesia where the sultan retains a real political role—the current sultan Hamengkubuwono X also serves as provincial governor. Bring a sarong for the most sacred areas.

⏱ 90 min
3

Pasar Beringharjo, a covered market steps from the Kraton, overflows with everything Java produces: intricately patterned batiks, sarongs, spices, medicinal herbs and tropical fruit. It's the ideal place to buy genuine handmade batik — recognizable by its slightly irregular patterns drawn by hand in hot wax. Machine-printed imitations cost ten times less but lose all their charm after the first wash. Take the time to haggle.

Activities

Gudeg dinner at a warung in the Kraton neighborhood. Gudeg is Yogyakarta's signature dish: young jackfruit slow-cooked for hours in coconut milk and palm sugar, served with rice, chicken and caramelized hard-boiled eggs. You won't find this dish anywhere else on Java prepared the same way. The warungs open early in the morning and close when the pot runs dry — sometimes before noon.

Yogyakarta

🛏️ Suggested base : Yogyakarta
★★★★★
1

Taman Sari, a 10-minute walk from the Kraton, is the sultan's water castle. Built in 1758 by Hamengkubuwono I, this complex served as a retreat and royal bathing grounds: three raised pools accommodated the sultan's wives and children, his concubines, and the sovereign himself respectively. From the turret overlooking the pools, the sultan could observe his wives bathing and make his choice. Today the pools are restored, but the rest of the complex—underground tunnels, ruined arches, secret passages—is still partly overgrown by vegetation. Hire a guide on site to unravel its history.

⏱ 75 min
2

Seventeen kilometers east of Yogyakarta, the temple of Prambanan is the largest Hindu complex in Indonesia. Built in the 9th century under the Sanjaya dynasty, it comprises 240 temples, the three main ones dedicated to Shiva, Vishnu and Brahma. The Shiva temple rises to 47 meters — its architectural plan draws on Mount Meru, residence of the gods in Hinduism. The walls are covered in bas-reliefs illustrating the Ramayana with a precision and attention to detail that would put many a European sculptor to shame. Arrive in the late afternoon: the sunset light on the gray stone spires is breathtaking.

⏱ 90 min

Activities

The Ramayana Ballet takes place outdoors in front of Prambanan's illuminated temples on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays during the dry season (May-October), at 7:30 p.m. More than 200 dancers and gamelan musicians perform the epic of Rama, Sita and Hanuman - a story first staged at Prambanan in 1961. The show lasts about 2 hours. During the rainy season, it moves indoors to the Trimurti stage. Book ahead in high season.

⏱ 120 min

Batik-making workshop in a studio in the Kraton quarter—the workshops on Taman Sari street and in Kota Gede teach the basics in 2 to 3 hours. You'll learn to sketch the pattern in pencil, apply hot wax with a tjanting (the traditional tool), then dip the fabric in dye. You'll take home your own batik—imperfect but authentic. A great souvenir and a lovely way to understand why batik has been listed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2009.

⏱ 120 min

Yogyakarta

🛏️ Suggested base : Yogyakarta
★★★★★
1

Get up before dawn for the Punthuk Setumbu viewpoint: plan a day trip from Yogyakarta, followed by a walk up to the viewpoint. In clear weather, you will see Borobudur's stupas emerge from a sea of clouds at sunrise - one of the most striking sights in Southeast Asia. In the 19th century, Governor-General Raffles, told of a “great monument hidden in the jungle”, sent a Dutch engineer and 200 men to clear the site in 1814. After two months of work, Borobudur reappeared.

⏱ 90 min
2

Borobudur can be visited on a round-trip excursion from Yogyakarta. It is the world's largest Buddhist monument: 123 meters on each side and 42 meters high, built around 800 under the Sailendra dynasty - contemporary with Charlemagne. The temple consists of 9 stacked terraces (6 square, 3 circular), topped by a central stupa. Its walls feature 2,672 bas-relief panels depicting the Buddha's life along several kilometers of galleries. Around the circular terraces, 72 openwork stupas each house a statue of the Buddha. Following its UNESCO restoration between 1975 and 1982 - a project involving 600 people - the site has been listed as a World Heritage Site since 1991. Arrive early: groups start arriving after 9 a.m.

⏱ 120 min
3

On the road back to Yogyakarta, stop at Mendut Temple, 3 km from Borobudur. This small 8th-century temple houses three large, remarkably well-preserved seated Buddha statues. Borobudur, Pawon and Mendut are aligned on the same geographic axis and likely formed a pilgrimage circuit. Scholars still debate the relationship between these three temples.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

Cycle through the villages around Borobudur, as a round-trip excursion from Yogyakarta - local operators offer routes through rice fields, cocoa plantations and traditional houses on the Kedu plateau. It is the best way to see rural Javanese life at ground level: farmers working the soil by hand, women carrying offerings on their heads, children running after visitors' bicycles. Allow 3 to 4 hours and bring water.

⏱ 180 min

Every year in May or June, on the full moon, Indonesian Buddhists celebrate Waisak at Borobudur — commemorating the birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha. Thousands of pilgrims walk by torchlight from Mendut to Borobudur. If your visit coincides with this period, it's a rare spectacle worth the effort. Check the exact dates in advance.

⏱ 120 min

Banyuwangi

🛏️ Suggested base : Banyuwangi
★★★☆☆
1

Long transfer day from Yogyakarta to Banyuwangi, at the far eastern tip of Java. The journey by car or train (Banyuwangi station has good connections from Surabaya and Yogyakarta) follows Java's south coast, passing through rice paddies and coffee plantations that gradually hint at the wilder atmosphere of the island's east. Banyuwangi is the gateway town for the Kawah Ijen ascent—get your gear ready as soon as you arrive.

⏱ 60 min
2

Kawah Ijen is one of only two places on Earth where "blue flames" can be observed — sulfurous gases that ignite on contact with oxygen at temperatures reaching 600°C. The phenomenon is visible only in darkness, between 2 and 4 AM. The crater sits at 2,386 meters and holds the world's largest acid lake: 45 hectares, 1 km long, with a pH hovering around 0.2 — the most acidic water on the planet. Three hundred miners descend into this crater every night to extract sulfur by hand, carrying loads of up to 90 kg.

Ubud

🛏️ Suggested base : Ubud
★★★★☆
1

After the night ascent of Ijen, take the ferry from Banyuwangi (Ketapang port) to Gilimanuk on the island of Bali—a crossing of roughly 30 to 45 minutes. Arrival in Bali by late morning, then drive to Ubud (about 2 hours 30 minutes east). Along the way, start to feel the difference: temple stelae adorn every intersection, flower and incense offerings are laid at dawn outside every shop, rice terraces step up the hillsides. Ubud has been Bali's artistic and spiritual center for centuries.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Campuhan Ridge Walk starts 2 km from downtown Ubud, near the Gunung Lebah temple. The trail follows a ridge between the Wos and Cerik rivers for 2 km, through elephant grass fields and fluorescent-green rice paddies. Set out before 8 AM to beat the heat and the organized groups. By mid-afternoon, this trail turns into a tourist parade — a different experience entirely.

⏱ 90 min
3

Pura Taman Saraswati, in the heart of Ubud, is a temple dedicated to the goddess of knowledge, surrounded by a lotus-covered pond that reflects the late-day light. It's one of the most photographed temples in Bali—arrive at sunset for the light, but wear a sarong as ceremonies are held regularly. The neighboring Ubud Royal Palace hosts Legong and Barong dance performances every evening at 7:30 PM.

Ubud

🛏️ Suggested base : Ubud
★★★★☆
1

The Tegallalang rice terraces are 12 km north of Ubud—20 minutes by motorcycle or scooter. Inscribed as UNESCO cultural heritage for the subak irrigation system (2012), these staircase terraces have been collectively managed by the village farmers for centuries: if an irrigation canal clogs, everyone pitches in to fix it. Get there before 9 AM—bus groups arrive by the dozens after that.

⏱ 90 min
2

Pura Tirta Empul, 15 km northeast of Ubud, is one of the most sacred temples in Bali. Its name means "sacred spring." Founded in 926 AD under the Warmadewa dynasty, it's built around a natural spring feeding 30 water jets in two ritual pools. According to legend, the god Indra struck the earth with his lance to create a life-giving elixir to save his warriors poisoned by the evil king Mayadenawa. You can take part in the Melukat ritual — purification under the water jets — wearing a specific sarong rented on site. Avoid the 11 AM–2 PM window, the busiest time.

⏱ 90 min
3

The Sacred Monkey Forest Sanctuary in Ubud is home to over 700 free-roaming crab-eating macaques across 12.5 hectares. Three 14th-century temples are nestled in the heart of the forest. The monkeys are used to visitors but won't hesitate to snatch glasses, cameras and food—stow everything in your pockets. Allow 1 hour 30 minutes to explore the various trails.

⏱ 75 min

Activities

Rafting on the Ayung River—Ubud's main river offers class 2–3 rapids along a roughly 10 km descent. Operators run 2-hour guided outings with gear included. The canyon you'll travel through is lined with rock walls covered in dense tropical vegetation and a few small waterfalls. A good way to see the Ubud valley from its floor.

⏱ 150 min

Balinese cooking class in a home or garden in Ubud — several formats are available including a visit to the local market at dawn to choose the ingredients, then preparation of 4 to 6 traditional dishes: sate lilit (spiced fish skewers), lawar, babi guling. The lesson ends at the table. It's one of the best ways to understand the spices that define Balinese culinary identity.

Uluwatu

🛏️ Suggested base : Ubud · 41 km

1

Padang Padang Beach is reached by a stairway squeezed between two limestone cliffs at the bottom of a rocky cleft—its natural simplicity contrasts with the buzz on the beach itself. The waters are remarkably clear and the beach, small and sheltered by the cliffs, offers waves suited to intermediate surfing. Arrive before 10 AM to claim a spot on the sand.

2

Pura Luhur Uluwatu stands 70 meters above the Indian Ocean on the limestone cliffs of the Bukit's southwestern tip. A temple founded in the 11th century by the Javanese sage Mpu Kuturan, it's one of the six “spiritual pillars” of Bali. Follow the coastal path around to see the building from below, looking up from the cliffs. Watch out for the temple's macaques—notorious for snatching glasses and jewelry: stow everything before entering.

Activities

The Kecak dance at Uluwatu is performed every evening at 6 PM on a stage clinging to the clifftop, with the Indian Ocean as a backdrop. The Kecak was created in the 1930s by German painter Walter Spies, then based in Bali, who drew on a Javanese male trance ritual to compose this choral spectacle: dozens of men seated in a circle chant "cak-cak-cak" without any instruments while costumed dancers perform episodes of the Ramayana against the setting sun. Arrive 30 minutes early to choose your seat in the stands.

⏱ 90 min

Seafood dinner on Jimbaran Beach, 20 minutes from Uluwatu. Dozens of tables are set up directly on the sand facing the sea, with vendors offering fish, shrimp and squid grilled to order. It's an institution in southern Bali. Arrive before 7 PM to grab a good spot facing the water and the lights of the bay.

Nusa Lembongan

🛏️ Suggested base : Nusa Lembongan
★★★★☆
1

Fast ferry from Sanur port to Nusa Lembongan—a crossing of roughly 35 to 45 minutes. On arrival, you'll find an island with almost no cars, with narrow roads lined with bougainvillea. Start by circling the island on foot or by motorcycle or scooter: Dream Beach first, a white-sand beach in a cove wedged between coral cliffs, with very clear but sometimes rough water. Watch out for currents—swimming is inadvisable in heavy swell.

⏱ 75 min
2

Devil's Tears is a series of blowholes — cavities in the coastal cliff where waves surge in and shoot skyward with a deep rumble. The name supposedly comes from the "tears" of mist these jets propel into the air. It's one of the most dramatic sites in the entire Bali region. Stay well back from the edge — the waves are unpredictable and several accidents have occurred. Sunset here is nothing short of breathtaking.

⏱ 60 min
3

The Yellow Bridge (yellow bridge) links Nusa Lembongan to the island of Nusa Ceningan—40 meters of suspended walkway above a turquoise lagoon, accessible on foot or by motorcycle or scooter. On Nusa Ceningan, the coastal road offers fine views of seaweed drying on ropes stretched above the lagoon—an important industry on the island.

Gili Air

🛏️ Suggested base : Gili Air
★★★★☆
1

Speedboat from Nusa Lembongan to Gili Air. Gili Air is the closest of the three Gili Islands to Lombok: no motor vehicles operate here, and you get around on foot, by bicycle or by cidomo (horse-drawn carriage). Walk around the island in 1 hour 30 minutes to get your bearings. The east coast has the best turtle snorkeling spots; the west coast offers the most spectacular sunsets.

⏱ 75 min
2

The Turtle Spot on Gili Air is marked by small wooden signs on the east coast. Swim about ten meters from the beach to the seagrass beds where green and hawksbill turtles come to feed. Visibility averages 20 to 30 meters. Stay 2–3 meters from the turtles when they surface to breathe. Morning at high tide is the best window — after 11 AM small jellyfish can appear on the north coast.

Labuan Bajo

🛏️ Suggested base : Labuan Bajo
★★★★☆
1

Departure from Labuan Bajo port at 6 AM by boat to Padar Island. The climb to the summit (30 to 45 minutes) reveals one of the most singular views in Indonesia: three bays separated by razor-sharp ridges, with waters of different colors depending on the angle and the hour. The Komodo Islands form a landscape of arid volcanic savanna that few regions on Earth can match.

⏱ 90 min
2

The Komodo dragon (Varanus komodoensis) is the largest living lizard in the world: 2.60 m on average, 80 to 90 kg. It is endemic to the islands of Komodo, Rinca and a few small neighboring islands — nowhere else on Earth. Its wild population is estimated at fewer than 3,500 individuals, classified as "Endangered" by the IUCN. On Komodo or Rinca Island, a ranger escort is mandatory. The dragons can sprint at 20 km/h over short distances and their bite is venomous — follow the ranger's instructions to the letter.

⏱ 120 min

Activities

Snorkeling at Pink Beach on Komodo Island—the beach owes its pink sand to red coral fragments mixed with white sand. The water is transparent, and the pink sandy bottom extends beneath the waves. The coral reefs immediately accessible from the beach shelter schools of colorful fish, turtles and, with luck, whitetip reef sharks at the base of the drop-off.

⏱ 90 min

Snorkeling at Manta Point—one of the best spots for swimming with manta rays in Southeast Asia. Mantas can reach 4 to 5 meters in wingspan and cruise through plankton-rich currents. Encounters are never guaranteed but chances are good between December and March (50 to 100 mantas recorded in a single day) and more hit-or-miss in the dry season. Swimming without fins is recommended to avoid disturbing the animals.
