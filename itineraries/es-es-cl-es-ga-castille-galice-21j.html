Segovia

🛏️ Suggested base : Segovia
★★★★☆
1

Welcome to Segovia. The city's silhouette is recognizable from afar: some say it resembles a ship. The Alcázar as the bow, the cathedral as the mainmast, the aqueduct as an anchor cast into the sierra. The Roman aqueduct is where you arrive: 167 arches, 28 meters high at its highest point, dry-laid granite—not a drop of mortar along the 800 meters still visible. Built around 50 CE, more likely under Domitian than Trajan. For the best photo, climb the Postigo stairs on the right: you'll find yourself level with the water channel, and it's from there that you grasp the brilliant absurdity of the undertaking.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Alcázar, perched at the tip of the promontory, did inspire Walt Disney's castle for Snow White—he visited it in 1935. Built from the 11th century on former Roman fortifications, expanded in the 13th by the kings of Castile, ravaged by fire in 1862 and rebuilt immediately afterward in fairy-tale fashion. Inside you'll find the throne room, the chamber of the Catholic Monarchs where Isabella was proclaimed queen in 1474, and—for the truly brave—152 spiral steps up to the Tower of Juan II. The view looks down over the Sierra de Guadarrama and the convents of the Eresma valley.

⏱ 90 min
3

The cathedral, nicknamed "the Lady of Cathedrals" for its elegance, is the last Gothic cathedral built in Spain—construction began in 1525 under Charles V after the previous one burned during the Comuneros revolt in 1520. Latin cross plan, 18 chapels, spectacular 16th-century Flemish stained glass. In the Concepción chapel, Ignacio de Ries's Tree of Life is a macabre curiosity: a giant crucifix as tree root, the damned falling to hell from the lower branches. Far from joyful.

⏱ 60 min
4

The Church of Vera Cruz, 10 minutes downhill from the Alcázar (then plan for the climb back), is one of the city's best-kept secrets. Dodecagonal plan—twelve sides—built by the Templars in 1208 and consecrated April 13 of that year by papal decree of Innocent III. At its center, a two-story shrine where knights kept vigil over a relic of the True Cross. It's rare in Europe, and that's what makes it remarkable: Vera Cruz has barely changed since the 13th century. Further on, the Parral monastery is under permanent restoration but accessible.

⏱ 45 min
5

On Plaza Mayor, facing the cathedral, don't miss Casa de los Picos—“House of Spikes”—a Renaissance façade bristling with 617 diamond-cut granite points, designed by the Marquises of Quintanar in the late 15th century. Calle Juan Bravo brings together Segovia's finest façades: Renaissance sgraffito with geometric motifs, aristocratic palaces, Casa de la Tierra, Torreón de Lozoya. Five minutes on foot between all these sights. Everything in Segovia fits within two kilometers.

Roast suckling pig—cochinillo asado—is Segovia's gastronomic institution. The benchmark: Mesón de Cándido, steps from the aqueduct, serving the dish since 1786. Tradition dictates that the maître d' carves the pig with the edge of a plate to prove it's cooked to perfection—tender enough not to need a knife—then breaks the plate on the floor. Locals take their evening stroll here: Segovians are so accustomed to their city that they forget about it even as they love it.

Avila

🛏️ Suggested base : Avila
★★★★☆
1

At an altitude of 1,131 meters, Ávila is one of Spain's highest cities—and you feel it in winter, when it often snows. The city reveals itself first through its ramparts: 2.5 kilometers of intact wall, 88 semicircular towers, 9 monumental gates. Built from 1080 on the orders of Raymond of Burgundy, son-in-law of Alfonso VI of Castile and León, completed in 1101. It's the best-preserved medieval wall in Europe—it nearly got demolished in the 19th century because it was thought to stifle urban expansion. Fortunately, the plan was abandoned. You can walk along 1,700 meters of the wall walk.

⏱ 90 min
2

On the wall itself, the Cathedral of the Savior. It gave architectural vocabulary the term “fortress-cathedral”. Its apse, nicknamed the Cimorro, literally forms part of the defensive system—it's one of the rampart towers, pierced with arrow slits. Spain's first Gothic cathedral, begun around 1170 by Master Fruchel, a Burgundian architect influenced by Saint-Denis. Built in oxblood-red sandstone that gives it its unique color. Inside, the main altarpiece by Pedro Berruguete and Juan de Borgoña, the tomb of El Tostado (the bishop and writer who published 50 volumes in 25 years and died in 1455).

⏱ 75 min
3

Outside the walls, five minutes on foot from Puerta del Alcázar, the Basilica of San Vicente is one of Spain's finest Romanesque buildings. Built between the 11th and 13th centuries on the site where saints Vincent, Sabina, and Christeta were allegedly martyred in 304 under Diocletian. The stone tomb-cenotaph at the nave's center recounts their martyrdom in moving sculpted bas-reliefs: flagellation, torture, embalming by a converted Jewish lady. West façade with its grand portico, historiated capitals, golden light in the morning. A stop on the Camino del Sur.

⏱ 60 min
4

You cannot understand Ávila without Teresa. Born here in 1515 to a family of converted Jewish merchants, she reformed the Carmelite order, founded 17 monasteries, wrote The Interior Castle, and became the first female Doctor of the Church in 1970. Her birthplace was transformed in the 17th century into a convent and baroque basilica. The adjoining museum displays some strange relics: a finger of the saint preserved in a monstrance, her cane, autograph manuscripts. More moving: the Monastery of the Incarnation, outside the walls, where she lived for 27 years and experienced the famous “transverberation” (a vision in which a seraph pierces her heart).

⏱ 90 min
5

For the postcard photo of Ávila, exit through Puerta del Carmen and follow the road northwest toward Salamanca for 1.5 km on foot to the Cuatro Postes. Four Doric columns topped by a cross, erected in 1566 on the path where, according to legend, young Teresa was caught by her uncle while running away with her brother to seek martyrdom among the Moors. Panoramic view of the illuminated ramparts, especially late in the day. That's where you take the best photos of Ávila.

⏱ 30 min

Activities

Sample yemas de Santa Teresa, an Ávila specialty since 1860. Small round balls made of beaten egg yolk and sugar, slowly cooked, presented in paper cups. Too sweet for many, but they define the city's sweet treats. The places to try: La Flor de Castilla confectionery, and the keeper of the original recipe's secret, Antigua Confitería La Talavera. Three yemas are enough—they're dense.

⏱ 30 min

Dine on chuletón de Ávila—the charcoal-grilled beef rib steak that is the province's emblem. Cattle of the avileña-negra ibérica breed are raised in the mountains of the Sierra de Gredos and produce marbled meat; they are slaughtered at an average age of 5–6 years (versus 18–24 months elsewhere). It's one of Spain's most tender meats. Restaurant El Almacén or Las Cancelas for the classic version, served rare. Allow €60 for two. If you happen upon the Jornadas Medievales in early September, the city transforms into a medieval market for three days.

⏱ 120 min

Salamanca

🛏️ Suggested base : Salamanca
★★★★★
1

Salamanca's Plaza Mayor is considered Spain's most beautiful baroque square—and it's hard to argue. Commissioned in 1729 by Philip V in gratitude to the city for its support in the War of Succession, designed by Alberto de Churriguera, completed in 1755. 88 arcades on three sides, the fourth occupied by the town hall. Look for the stone medallions in the spandrels: Christopher Columbus, Cervantes, Cortés, Franco (yes, removed and restored depending on the era). The local sandstone turns orange at sunset—hence the nickname "golden city."

⏱ 45 min
2

The University of Salamanca, founded in 1218 by Alfonso IX of León, is Spain's oldest university and Europe's fourth oldest (after Bologna, Paris, Oxford). Christopher Columbus defended his project here in 1486; Antonio de Nebrija published the first Spanish grammar here in 1492. The Plateresque façade of the Escuelas Mayores (1529) is so finely carved it looks like a goldsmith's work in stone. The challenge: find the lucky frog on one of the three skulls in the pediment. Students say you'll pass your exams if you spot it on your own. Hint: on the right, on the rightmost skull. Tell no one.

⏱ 75 min
3

Salamanca has two adjoining cathedrals. The Romanesque Catedral Vieja, dating from the 12th century, preserves in its apse an altarpiece of 53 panels painted by Nicolás Florentino around 1445—one of Spain's largest Gothic ensembles, completed by a fresco of the Last Judgment. The late Gothic and later baroque Catedral Nueva was begun in 1513 without destroying the old one—a rare occurrence. A curiosity: on the north façade (Puerta de Ramos), an astronaut and a faun eating ice cream were carved in 1992 during a restoration. Look closely.

⏱ 90 min
4

Facing the university, the Casa de las Conchas—"House of Shells." Built between 1493 and 1517 by Don Rodrigo Maldonado de Talavera, university professor and knight of the Order of Santiago. The façade is adorned with 365 carved scallop shells (one for each day of the year), symbol of the order to which he belonged. Today a public library, free access to the interior courtyard and first floor. According to a legend at least four centuries old, one of the shells hides an ounce of gold. No one has ever found it. And no one has the right to look underneath.

A tapas crawl along Calle Van Dyck or around Plaza Mayor. Salamanca is one of Spain's tapas capitals—here tapas are still served free with your drink, unlike in Madrid or Barcelona. Try the hornazo (meat pie with serrano ham, chorizo and hard-boiled egg), chanfaina (ratatouille with rice and beef tongue), and especially the ibérico ham from Guijuelo, 30 km from Salamanca—one of Spain's best PDO products, from pigs raised on acorns in the oak groves of the Sierra de Béjar.

Salamanca

🛏️ Suggested base : Salamanca
★★★★★
1

The Convent of San Esteban, ten minutes from Plaza Mayor, is one of Salamanca's finest Plateresque buildings—a 30-meter-high stone façade resembling an altarpiece, carved by Juan de Ribero between 1524 and 1610, with a relief depicting the Stoning of Saint Stephen. A Dominican convent where Christopher Columbus stayed in 1486 during negotiations with university theologians. The church's main altarpiece by José de Churriguera (1693) is a 27-meter-high baroque explosion. The Renaissance cloister, by Juan de Álava, is a haven of calm.

⏱ 75 min
2

Right next door, the Convent of Las Dueñas has an irregular pentagonal cloister. Built in the 16th century for Dominican nuns, it is still home to 9 cloistered sisters who sell pastries through a revolving hatch (you speak through the wall, put the money in the hatch, it turns, and the cookies arrive). Above all, the cloister capitals: grotesque sculptures, demons and monsters, unusual for a convent—scholars still disagree about their meaning.

⏱ 45 min
3

Casa Lis, above the Tormes against the old walls, houses the Museum of Art Nouveau and Art Deco, one of Spain's best. The house itself, built in 1905 by modernist Joaquín de Vargas, is a marvel—multicolored stained glass flooding the hall, floral ironwork, marble and bronze staircase. Collection: 19,000 pieces including porcelain dolls by Bru and Jumeau, bronzes by Demêtre Chiparus, Émile Gallé furniture. Very intimate visit, never crowded.

⏱ 75 min
4

The Roman Bridge—Puente Mayor del Tormes—dates from the 1st century CE and was part of the Vía de la Plata, the Roman road linking Mérida to Astorga. The southern half was rebuilt in the 17th century after a flood. At the bridge's end, a pre-Roman sculpture—perhaps a Vettonian verraco, perhaps an Iberian bull—over 2,000 years old. The bridge is pedestrian, perfect for photographing both cathedrals from the other bank. Lazarillo de Tormes, Spain's first picaresque novel (1554), begins precisely when Lazarillo's master forces him to ram his head against this stone.

⏱ 30 min

Activities

If the sky is clear, a hot-air balloon flight at sunrise over Salamanca—the outing starts around 6 a.m., lasts one hour, and offers an unmatched view of the two cathedrals and the Tormes plain. Several operators (Aerotours Castilla, Globos Boreal), from €175. The whisper of the burner, low sunlight on the sandstone, and traditionally a countryside brunch with cava on your return. Reservations are essential in season.

⏱ 240 min

Walk in Barrio del Oeste, behind the train station. Post-Franco working-class neighborhood that has metamorphosed into an open-air street art gallery since 2013—Galería Urbana festival. Over 100 murals by international muralists: Kraser Tres, Phlegm, Borondo. It's the perfect antithesis to the golden sandstone of the center. Pick up the free map at the Plaza Mayor tourist office.

Zamora

🛏️ Suggested base : Zamora
★★★★☆
1

On the road from Salamanca, a recommended stop at Toro—a 30 km detour via the N-122. Toro PDO vineyards and the 12th-century Romanesque-Byzantine Collegiate Church of Santa María la Mayor, with its golden octagonal façade. If time is tight, go straight to Zamora. Otherwise, a quick tasting at Bodegas Liberalia or Estancia Piedra is enough to understand why Toro wines have been gaining prominence for 20 years: tannic tinta de toro, plenty of structure, perfect with Castilian meats.

⏱ 60 min
2

Zamora is nicknamed “the pearl of the Duero” and “the city of Romanesque art”. With 24 Romanesque churches from the 12th–13th centuries, it has the world's greatest urban concentration of Romanesque architecture. The cathedral, founded by Alfonso VII and consecrated in 1175 after 25 years of construction, is a unique blend of Romanesque, Byzantine, and Herrerian styles. Its lantern tower—the cimborio—is its signature feature: a 16-sided dome covered with stone scales like a Japanese mushroom, inspired by Constantinople's Hagia Sophia via the Crusaders. Don't miss the Bishop's Door, one of Spain's finest Romanesque portals.

⏱ 90 min
3

The cathedral museum preserves one of the world's most important collections of Flemish tapestries—12 pieces from the 15th and 16th centuries, workshops of Arras, Tournai, and Brussels. The Trojan War series (1480), 4 by 7 meters each, recounts the fate of Hector and Achilles with a thousand figures embroidered in gold thread. Acquired by Zamoran bishops at mind-boggling prices, they survived the Napoleonic Wars and the Civil War. It's the textile equivalent of the Sistine Chapel—and nobody queues here.

⏱ 60 min
4

Five minutes on foot, the 11th-century castle, restored, today houses a public park—free entry, panoramic view over the Duero and stone bridge. Further on, the Church of San Pedro y San Ildefonso (12th century) guards the tomb of Saint Ildefonsus, Archbishop of Toledo in the 7th century, whose relics were transferred here in the 12th to protect them from the Moors. La Magdalena, on Calle Damas, has an exceptionally ornate Romanesque portal—one of Spain's finest, finely worked with floral motifs and figures.

⏱ 75 min
5

The stone bridge—Puente de Piedra—is Zamora's symbol. Built in the 12th century on the remains of a Roman bridge, 16 arches, 320 meters long. It was the obligatory Duero crossing for 800 years. From here you get the best view of the old town's silhouette at dusk. Locals do their evening jog here and stop to watch lights come on one by one on the façades. Walk along the banks (Senda del Duero) to the Aceñas de Olivares, ancient 12th-century water mills.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

If you are here during Holy Week, Zamora holds one of Spain's most impressive celebrations, declared a Festival of International Tourist Interest. Seventeen brotherhoods and 50,000 nazarenos parade in pointed hoods through silent streets lit only by their candles. The Cristo de las Injurias, a 16th-century sculpture, is carried in procession in absolute silence at 11:30 p.m. on Holy Wednesday—even coughing is forbidden.

⏱ 120 min

The Ethnographic Museum of Castile and León, 5 minutes from the center, is one of northern Spain's most surprising museums. Four thematic floors—space, time, forms, body—tracing Castilian rural ways of life before industrialization. Tools, costumes, objects used in transhumance, toys; modern museum displays that are anything but dusty. Allow 1.5 hours. A museum you leave feeling you've learned something, which isn't so common in the provinces.

Puebla De Sanabria

🛏️ Suggested base : Puebla De Sanabria
★★★☆☆
1

Puebla de Sanabria is one of Spain's most beautiful villages—an official designation—perched at 960 meters at the confluence of the Tera and Castro, 20 km from the Portuguese border. In the 13th century, it was one of the Kingdom of León's most important military strongholds facing Portugal. The Counts of Benavente owned it for four centuries. The main cobbled lane winds uphill between houses with stone coats of arms, flowering gardens, and wrought-iron balconies. You can cross the center in 30 minutes, but you'll spend the morning there.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Counts of Benavente's castle crowns the town—15th century, square plan, multistory keep nicknamed “El Macho”. One of Castile's best-preserved castles. Restored, it now houses an interpretation center on fortifications across four levels: models, videos, interactive games about the military history of the Portuguese-Castilian border. Ideal for children. From the keep, a panoramic view over the Sierra de la Culebra and—on clear days—as far as the peaks of Portugal.

⏱ 75 min
3

On Plaza Mayor, the Church of Nuestra Señora del Azogue—late 12th century, transitional Romanesque and Gothic. Three semicircular apses, a 13th-century baptismal font adorned with carved figures, a Renaissance altarpiece. “Azogue” comes from the Arabic name for the market held here. The Virgin of Victories, inside, is the town's patron saint—feast day on September 8, with a procession through the medieval streets featuring giants and big-headed figures. Less conspicuous: the Museum of Giants and Big-Headed Figures next door displays processional figures dating from the 19th century onward.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

An excursion to Lake Sanabria, 12 km north—the Iberian Peninsula's largest glacial lake, 369 hectares, 50 meters deep, formed 100,000 years ago by retreating glaciers. It even has small “waves” in strong winds, like an inland sea. Sandy beaches are an option in summer (Viquiella and Custa Llago). Outside summer, hike to Laguna de los Peces—a high-altitude lake at 1,707 meters—or to the Sotillo waterfalls. The water is icy all year, even in August.

⏱ 240 min

15 minutes south, the Iberian Wolf Center in Robledo (Centro del Lobo Ibérico) takes in injured wild wolves and wolves born in captivity. Several observation trails in natural surroundings, with viewing huts, let you see the packs from a distance without disturbing them. The neighboring Sierra de la Culebra is home to Western Europe's largest wolf population. Open only at certain hours for guided tours; reservations are required in season. Moving, especially for children.

Ourense

🛏️ Suggested base : Ourense
★★★★☆
1

Ourense—the city takes on a different face as you enter Galicia. The Romans founded it at the end of the 1st century BCE under the name Aquae Urentes (“burning waters”) or Auriense (“city of gold”), for its thermal springs and the gold then found in the Miño. Today it is Galicia's third-largest city and one of the few in Europe with thermal waters in the heart of the city. Its focal point is As Burgas, three springs that gush out at 67°C with a flow of 300 liters per minute. The Romans had already built a thermal sanctuary dedicated to the nymphs here—the excavations are visible.

⏱ 60 min
2

Five minutes' walk away, the Cathedral of San Martiño dates from the 12th–13th centuries. Built on a 6th-century Suevic church. The centerpiece is the Pórtico do Paraíso: a later copy (13th century) of Santiago's Pórtico da Gloria by Master Mateo, but one that has preserved its original polychromy—exceedingly rare, since the original in Compostela was stripped. Here you see what Romanesque portals must have looked like before centuries wore away their colors. Inside, the Cristo de Ourense, a 14th-century polychrome sculpture with human hair, is the object of local devotion.

⏱ 60 min
3

The Ponte Vella spans the Miño with its seven arches, the central arch rising 40 meters above the water. Roman foundations from the reign of Augustus, medieval reconstruction in 1230 by Bishop Lourenzo, restorations in the 17th and 19th centuries. Today closed to cars, it's Spain's oldest bridge to have retained such a uniform silhouette. Beside it, the Ponte do Milenio (2001) provides a contemporary architectural counterpart—a pedestrian walkway suspended 22 meters above the river, accessible by stairs. Panoramic view of both banks.

⏱ 45 min
4

The San Francisco cloister, behind the former convent, is considered Galicia's finest Gothic cloister. Built in the 14th century, 63 pointed arches, 76 double columns, 120 different capitals—each carved with a unique motif: foliage, fantastic animals, anthropomorphic scenes. The Franciscan convent was dismantled in the 19th century, the cloister transferred stone by stone to its current location to save it from destruction. Absolute calm, free access, few visitors.

Activities

Pontevedra

🛏️ Suggested base : Pontevedra
★★★★☆
1

Pontevedra is a revelation. A city of 80,000 inhabitants, the provincial capital, and above all: a listed historic center that has been entirely pedestrianized since 1999. Not a single motor vehicle in the entire old town—one of Spain's most radical urban mobility projects, recognized with several international awards. The result: quality of life, café terraces everywhere, children playing in the streets, an almost zero accident rate. The center is organized around five interconnected squares: Ferrería, Curros Enríquez, Leña, Verdura, Teucro.

⏱ 60 min
2

25 km north of Pontevedra, an excursion to Cambados is a must for anyone interested in Galician wine. The town is the capital of Albariño, the fresh, mineral white wine that has become one of Spain's great whites in thirty years. The center is clustered around Praza de Fefiñáns, designated a national monument: the Renaissance Pazo de Fefiñáns (1535–1647) with its baroque arch spanning the street, the late Gothic Church of San Bieito, and the 14th-century defensive tower. Uneven cobblestones, wine merchants under the arcades. Sublime at sunset.

⏱ 75 min
3

In Cambados, on Monte da Pastora hill 10 minutes from the center, the ruins of Santa Mariña Dozo. A 16th-century Gothic church abandoned in the 19th century and left to fall into ruin. But the ground between the collapsed arches still serves as the parish cemetery: tombstones set in the grass between broken columns, vaults open to the sky, lichens, moss. The most photogenic place in all of Galicia, and one of the most moving. Freely accessible, never crowded. View over the Ría de Arousa.

⏱ 45 min
4

The Church of La Peregrina, at the entrance to the historic center, has a unique shape: an octagonal cross plan, with angles of 45° rather than 90°. Built in the 18th century (1778), its style is midway between baroque and neoclassical. Its symbolic shape: the scallop shell. Pontevedra was a stop on the Camino Portugués, and La Peregrina is the patron saint of the Portuguese Way. The interior is equally unexpected—small, centrally planned, bright. An 18th-century statue of the Virgin in polychrome wood, venerated by sailors.

⏱ 30 min
5

The Basilica of Santa María la Mayor, overlooking the Lérez River, has a Plateresque façade carved by Cornelis de Hollanda (1541–1559)—Renaissance ornamentation with the delicacy of a goldsmith's work. Inside, a late Gothic hall-church plan, baroque altarpieces, and the curious “capilla del Cristo del Buen Viaje”—Christ of the Good Voyage, an object of devotion for sailors heading out to sea. Funded by the sailors' guild at the height of Pontevedra's commercial prosperity in the 16th century. The city was then larger than Vigo or A Coruña.

⏱ 60 min
6

Fine arts: Castelao (cartoonist and painter, Galician cultural hero), Sotomayor, Flemish painting. Santiago De Compostela

🛏️ Suggested base : Santiago De Compostela
★★★★☆
1

Santiago de Compostela—Santiago in Galician. The end of the Camino, where hundreds of thousands of pilgrims arrive each year. Praza do Obradoiro provides a scenic arrival: on the right, the cathedral and its baroque façade by Fernando de Casas Novoa (1738–1750), that stone filigree whose very name—obradoiro—means “goldsmith” in Galician; on the left, the Hostal dos Reis Católicos, a royal hospital for pilgrims founded in 1499 by the Catholic Monarchs, now a 5-star Parador; opposite, the Pazo de Raxoi (18th-century neoclassical, city hall and Galician government); to the south, the Pazo de San Xerome, the university rector's offices.

⏱ 60 min
2

The cathedral is dedicated to James the Greater, whose body, according to tradition, was found in a field around 813 by the hermit Pelagius, guided by a star (“campus stellae” → Compostela). Three successive cathedrals: the first built by Alfonso II in 829, burned by al-Mansur in 997, rebuilt in pre-Romanesque style by Alfonso III, and replaced by the current Romanesque building begun in 1075 under Alfonso VI. The Pórtico da Gloria—a Romanesque entrance portal carved by Master Mateo, completed in 1188—was restored in 2018 and regained its medieval polychromy. A separate paid visit (€15), but arguably Europe's finest Romanesque portal.

⏱ 90 min
3

The Botafumeiro is the image that gets shared: a giant censer 1.60 meters tall and weighing 54 kg, swinging at up to 68 km/h beneath the transept vault, 21 meters high. The tradition is documented as early as 1322. Eight tiraboleiros pull the ropes to make it swing until it almost brushes the vault. It is used during certain liturgical celebrations scheduled by the cathedral and can also be operated with a paid reservation.

⏱ 60 min
4

Tour the three other squares around the cathedral: Praza das Praterías to the south (with the fountain of the horses and the original 12th-century Romanesque façade), Praza da Quintana to the east (monumental staircase, eastern façade, Porta Santa, which opens only during Holy Years), Praza da Inmaculada to the north (entrance to the Monastery of San Martiño Pinario). Each square offers a different perspective on the cathedral—that's what makes Santiago's urban planning so clever. The cathedral is designed to be seen from all sides, never just from the front.

🛏️ Suggested base : Santiago De Compostela
★★★★☆
1

The Monastery of San Martiño Pinario, right beside the cathedral, is Spain's second-largest Benedictine monastery after El Escorial. Founded in the 9th century, rebuilt in the 16th–17th centuries in baroque-Renaissance style. The altarpiece-like façade by Bartolomé Fernández Lechuga (1606) is spectacular—50 meters high, carved like a cliff. Inside, two baroque altarpieces by Casas Novoa and Fernando de Casas, more impressive than the cathedral's according to many. Cloistered sisters still sell cookies through a revolving hatch. A quiet visit, €5, with no line.

⏱ 75 min
2

The Colexio de Fonseca, on Rúa do Franco, houses the university library. A Renaissance cloister by Alonso de Covarrubias (1532–1544), a perfect illustration of Spanish humanism. The University of Santiago, founded in 1495 by Archbishop Alonso III de Fonseca, is one of Spain's oldest. Its buildings are scattered throughout the city. Make a detour to the Pazo de San Xerome, on Obradoiro—a 15th-century façade, one of the few surviving traces of the original university.

⏱ 45 min
3

Alameda Park offers the finest panoramic view of the cathedral—from Paseo dos Leóns, at the western end. Camellias bloom from January to March (Galicia produces 80% of Europe's camellias), alongside century-old eucalyptus trees. Along the way, the baroque Church of Santa Susana and the sacred oak grove. This park was the green lung of the Compostelan bourgeoisie in the 19th century. The poet Rosalía de Castro came here to dream—her statue is at the entrance.

⏱ 45 min
4

For modern art lovers: the Cidade da Cultura de Galicia, on Monte Gaiás at the city's edge (5 km, taxi or bus). A contemporary complex designed by American architect Peter Eisenman, inaugurated in 2011—six buildings with undulating roofs that echo the contours of the ground and the route of the Camino. Three buildings are complete (Museum of Galicia, library, archives), while three remain unfinished for lack of funding (which has become a matter of public debate in Galicia). The complex is worth the detour for its architecture, even if its isolation makes it somewhat ghostly.

⏱ 90 min

Activities

Fisterra

🛏️ Suggested base : Fisterra
★★★☆☆
1

From Santiago, head west to the Costa da Morte—“Coast of Death”—so named for the hundreds of shipwrecks that have occurred since antiquity on this rocky coast with its powerful currents. Galicia's wildest coast: pink granite cliffs, deserted beaches, isolated lighthouses, fishing villages with low houses. Possible stops at Muros and Carnota before Finisterre, or a detour via Muxía for another legend associated with Saint James.

⏱ 60 min
2

Muxía, 30 km from Finisterre, is home to the Sanctuary of Virxe da Barca—“Virgin of the Boat”. According to legend, the Virgin appeared to James the Greater on this beach, arriving in a stone boat whose remains are still visible: the pedras de abalar (“rocking stone”), pedra dos cadrís (“stone of the kidneys”, under which people crawl to cure back pain, according to tradition). The baroque sanctuary (1719) burned after a lightning strike in 2013 and has since been restored. An evocative place, the end of the other branch of the Camino (Muxía–Fisterra).

⏱ 60 min
3

Cape Finisterre itself. A lighthouse built in 1853, 138 meters above the Atlantic. Here, for many pilgrims, their Camino ends—at km 0. A tradition (fairly recent, dating from the 1980s): burning your shoes or an item of clothing at sunset. This should no longer be done—it is prohibited, with fines for pollution. But the emotion remains: sitting on the rocks, with waves 100 m below and the sun dropping straight into the ocean, you understand why the Romans thought the world ended here—Finis Terrae.

⏱ 90 min
4

The village of Fisterra (Finisterre) itself deserves more than a brief visit. The historic center surrounds Praza de Ara Solis—named after the Celtic solar altar the Romans found here. The Church of Santa María das Areas (12th–14th centuries), at the foot of the path to the lighthouse, preserves the Santo Cristo da Barba Dourada—Christ with the golden beard, fishermen's legends and miracles. An active fishing port, with a fish market in the late afternoon. Langosteira Beach, immense and almost always deserted outside summer.

⏱ 60 min

Activities

Mar de Fora Beach, a 20-minute walk from the village, behind the hill. White sand, turquoise waters on sunny days—but often dangerous swells and no lifeguards. This is a place for contemplation rather than lounging. Legendary sunsets. Calmer: Praia da Langosteira, longer, on the edge of the village, more sheltered.

⏱ 120 min

A fish dinner in the village of Fisterra. The port has its own fish auction, and restaurants source their fish directly. Dishes: caldeirada de pescado (fish stew), arroz con bogavante (rice with lobster), navajas a la plancha (griddled razor clams), and pulpo a feira, of course. Places to try: O Centolo, O Fragón, Casa Velay. Allow €35–50 for a full meal. The fish comes off the boats that very morning.

A Coruna

🛏️ Suggested base : A Coruna
★★★★☆
1

A Coruña—its Galician name. A city of 245,000 inhabitants facing the Atlantic, with a more open and modern character than other Galician cities. Its nickname: the “city of glass”, for the glazed galleries on the façades along Avenida da Mariña—typical of the 19th century, designed to capture every ray of sunlight and provide shelter from sea winds. It's Galicia's most emblematic urban waterfront: follow the Mariña from the port to the Méndez Núñez gardens, a walk of about 1 km.

⏱ 60 min
2

The Tower of Hercules, 2.5 km north of the center. UNESCO-listed in 2009—it's the only Roman lighthouse still in operation since antiquity. Built in the late 1st century CE, with 34 meters of Roman construction plus 21 meters from the neoclassical restoration of 1791 by Eustaquio Giannini, totaling 55 meters. The farum Brigantium guided ships on the Atlantic sea route linking the Mediterranean to northern Europe. Celtic legend has it that Hercules buried the giant Geryon's head there. 234 steps to the summit, a 360° view over the Atlantic, with the Cíes Islands to the south on clear days.

⏱ 90 min
3

The sculpture park around the Tower of Hercules houses some emblematic works: Manolo Paz's Menhirs for Peace, Daniel Buren's Marine Snail, and especially the colossal statue of Breogán—a mythological Celtic hero who is said to have founded Brigantia (ancient A Coruña). The Monte dos Bicos petroglyphs, carved in the Iron Age, are a reminder that the site was sacred long before the Romans. Beside it, the 19th-century Muslim cemetery—surprising in A Coruña—where North African sailors who died in shipwrecks are buried. Moving.

⏱ 45 min
4

Praza de María Pita, the heart of the modern city, is named after the local heroine who is said to have saved the city from Francis Drake's English attack in May 1589. According to the chronicle, after her husband died defending the city, María Pita seized a lance, killed the English standard-bearer and drove the attackers away, shouting “Quien tenga honra, que me siga”. 160 by 100 meters, surrounded by arcades, with city hall (1909) at the center. On the sunny terraces, Coruñans have their coffee as soon as the sun appears—a rare commodity in a Galician winter.

Lugo

🛏️ Suggested base : Lugo
★★★★☆
1

Lugo, a 2,000-year-old city. Founded in 13 BCE under the name Lucus Augusti—“sacred grove of Augustus”—derived from the Celtic deity Lugh, to whom a sacred grove on the site was dedicated. And the city boils down to one thing absolutely unique in the world: its Roman wall. A circumference of 2,117 meters, 71 towers preserved out of the original 85, 10 gates (5 Roman, 5 added later). Built between 260 and 320 under Constantine I. It's the only Roman wall in Europe to have preserved its entire perimeter, which earned it UNESCO listing in 2000. The wall walk, 4 meters wide, can be followed on foot or by bike—free, open 24/7.

⏱ 90 min
2

Inside the walls, the Cathedral of Santa María. Construction began in 1129 on the orders of Alfonso VII and continued century after century in every style: a Romanesque nave, Gothic ambulatory, baroque west façade, neoclassical cloister. The standout feature: the Chapel of the Virgin of the Large Eyes (Virxe dos Ollos Grandes), spectacular Galician baroque. And a unique privilege in Spain: Lugo Cathedral has kept the Blessed Sacrament on perpetual display since the 14th century, following a Eucharistic miracle. It's depicted in the city's coat of arms—a chalice with a host.

⏱ 60 min
3

Beneath a pedestrian street in the center, the Casa dos Mosaicos is a recently opened archaeological site (2011): the remains of a 3rd-century Roman domus, with mosaics in situ. You see them through a glass floor. The rooms retain their layout: tablinum, oecus, peristylium. Digital reconstructions on screens. Small, surprising, free, never crowded. Five minutes away is Praza do Campo, a former Roman forum that became a medieval market, now the heart of tapas evenings.

⏱ 30 min
4

Outside the walls, 300 meters to the south, the ancient Roman baths are incorporated into the Balneario de Lugo hotel building—iron- and bicarbonate-rich water at 43°C that has flowed continuously since antiquity. The 1st-century changing room and cold-bath room have been preserved. You can bathe in the spa (allow €25 for admission; it is a proper spa). The Ponte Vello, which spans the Miño right next door, is also Roman in origin—rebuilt in the 12th century.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

A gastronomic tour. The official slogan “For food, Lugo” is no empty boast: the city has an exceptional concentration of starred or recommended restaurants. Local specialties: lacón con grelos (ham hock with turnip greens), pulpo a feira, tarta de Mondoñedo, Galician churrasco, PDO tetilla cheese. Don't miss a small draft beer of Estrella Galicia—a regional beer brewed in A Coruña—with a slice of empanada. Reliable places: Mesón Alberto, Paprica, Don Mansito.

⏱ 150 min

If you catch Arde Lucus (the weekend closest to the summer solstice, in mid-June), it's the event not to miss. For three days, the city transforms into ancient Rome: 600 costumed participants, legionaries, senators, slaves, gladiators, Roman weddings, banquets, reenacted battles in the forum. Over 300,000 visitors in three days. A Festival of National Tourist Interest since 2007.

★★★☆☆

A short trip from Lugo—105 km, 1 hour 20 minutes via the A-6. ⏱ 60 min
2

Astorga's Episcopal Palace is one of only three works by Gaudí outside Catalonia (the other two: Casa Botines in León, El Capricho in Comillas). Commissioned in 1887 by Bishop Joan Grau, who came from Reus like Gaudí. Construction began in 1889. When the bishop died in 1893, Gaudí clashed with the new diocesan authorities, stormed out in fury, swore never to return to Astorga, and kept his word. The building was completed in 1913 by Ricardo García Guereta. Neo-Gothic style through Gaudí's eyes—so anything but orthodox: turrets, battlements, and moats give it the character of a castle, but inside are bright, church-like spaces and multicolored stained glass recalling León Cathedral.

⏱ 90 min
3

The Cathedral of Santa María, right beside it, is an architectural layer cake: begun in 1471 on the foundations of a Romanesque church, completed in the 18th century. Hence this blend: a Renaissance-baroque façade, asymmetrical towers (one in pink-gray sandstone, the other in darker granite and struck by lightning several times), the main altarpiece by Gaspar Becerra (1558), one of the masterpieces of Spanish Mannerist sculpture. On a pinnacle of the apse, you can see the statue of a figure in Maragato costume—Pedro Mato, the subject of contradictory local legends.

⏱ 60 min
4

The Roman Museum, in the “Ergástula”—an ancient Roman underground structure, perhaps a holding area for slaves—provides access to the Ruta Romana, a 1.5-hour loop through the underground remains: Roman sewers, two bath complexes, the House of the Bear and Birds with its mosaics in situ. Gold from Las Médulas (60 km away, UNESCO-listed Roman mines) passed through Astorga—hence the opulence of its urban infrastructure from the 1st to the 3rd centuries.

⏱ 90 min

Activities

The Chocolate Museum. Astorga was one of Europe's chocolate capitals in the 19th–20th centuries: up to 49 factories operating simultaneously around 1914, processing cocoa unloaded in A Coruña. The museum displays old machines, vintage advertising posters, and archives. Cinnamon chocolate is still a specialty, along with mantecadas—small Maragato cakes. Don't miss Confiteria Velasco or La Maragata for a box to take home.

⏱ 60 min

Dinner of cocido maragato—a complete meal served in reverse order: first the meats (seven or nine: pork, beef, chicken, ham hock, pork fat, chorizo, blood sausage), then chickpeas and vegetables, and finally soup. The logic goes back to Maragato transhumance: people ate the most substantial food first in case of an alert. Casa Maragata I and II are the benchmarks—reservations are essential. Allow €25–30 per person, including wine.

Leon

🛏️ Suggested base : Leon
★★★★☆
1

León—the historic capital of the kingdom of the same name from the 10th to the 13th century. A city of 120,000 inhabitants, a major stop on the Camino Francés, and probably the finest concentration of heritage in Castile and León after Salamanca. Its local nickname: “la Pulchra Leonina”, referring to its cathedral. A compact historic center, crossed by the Bernesga, arranged around Plaza Mayor (1654, Castilian baroque, later than Madrid's but more intimate) and Plaza San Marcelo, where Casa Botines stands.

⏱ 60 min
2

León Cathedral is above all a story of stained glass. Its windows cover 1,800 m², with a remarkable proportion of glass to stone. Construction began in 1205, directly inspired by Reims and Amiens, and was completed in the 15th century. The naves almost buckle beneath the colored light—the Leonese “miracle of light”. The windows date from the 13th to the 20th century and undergo constant restoration. The west rose window is a model of perfect cathedral unity. Do not miss sunset, when the west nave glows.

⏱ 90 min
3

The Basilica of San Isidoro, a few streets away, is León's other jewel. A Romanesque church consecrated in 1063 by Ferdinand I the Great to house the remains of Isidore of Seville, brought from al-Andalus. Its signature feature: the Royal Pantheon—a small vaulted chapel right at the entrance, where 23 kings and queens, 12 infantes and 9 counts are buried. Its 12th-century murals in situ—an agricultural calendar, biblical scenes—are nicknamed “the Sistine Chapel of Romanesque art”. One of Europe's best collections of Romanesque mural painting, intact.

⏱ 90 min
4

The museum of the Collegiate Church of Saint Isidore preserves the Chalice of Doña Urraca—an agate cup set in gold and precious stones, donated by Queen Urraca in the 11th century. A study published in 2014 by two female historians identified the two pieces of agate forming the cup as the “Cup of Saint James” venerated by early Christians in Jerusalem—in other words, perhaps the cup used at the Last Supper. The theory is disputed. But it has doubled visitor numbers since. Could the Holy Grail be in León? See for yourself and judge.

⏱ 45 min
5

Casa Botines, on Plaza San Marcelo, is Gaudí's other work in Castile and León. Built in 1892–1893 for two textile merchants, Juan Homs and Mariano Andrés. Neo-Gothic style with modernist touches—a round tower at each of the four corners, pointed windows, a statue of Saint George slaying the dragon on the façade. Now a museum open to visitors—a reconstructed late-19th-century apartment, an exhibition on Gaudí, access to the upper floors with views over the square.

Leon

🛏️ Suggested base : Leon
★★★★☆
1

The Hostal de San Marcos, northwest of the center, is one of Spain's finest Plateresque complexes—a 100-meter façade with a frieze of sculpted busts of historical figures. Built from 1513 on the foundations of a 12th-century hospital for pilgrims, converted into a convent-monastery for the Knights of Saint James. Today: a 5-star Parador, one of Spain's most prestigious. You can visit the adjoining church, the cloister, and the provincial museum housed in the chapter house—all free. See the Christ of Carmel by Juan de Juni (1576) in the sacristy.

⏱ 75 min
2

MUSAC—Museo de Arte Contemporáneo de Castilla y León—is one of northern Spain's most distinctive contemporary art museums. A 2005 building by architects Mansilla & Tuñón, with a façade of 37 colored glass panels inspired by the cathedral's rose window (each color sampled from a medieval stained-glass window). The collection focuses on artists born after 1960. Often excellent temporary exhibitions. A 15-minute walk from the center, on Avenida de los Reyes Leoneses.

⏱ 90 min

Activities

The Palacio de los Guzmanes, opposite Casa Botines, is a 16th-century Renaissance palace that now houses the Provincial Council of León. Step into its inner courtyard: two levels of arcades, classical columns, galleries and the coats of arms of the Guzmán family. The façade stretches between four corner towers. A few minutes away on foot, Mercado del Conde Luna showcases produce from the province: cecina de León, cheeses, vegetables, cured meats and Bierzo wines. It is a tangible way to connect the city’s monumental heritage with its local food.

A closer alternative: the Cuevas de Valporquero (45 minutes north). A limestone cave system discovered in 1928, open to visitors, with immense chambers, stalactites, and an underground lake. A 1-hour guided tour, with a temperature of 7°C even in summer—a warm layer is essential. Adapted passageways, accessible to families. Nearby, hiking in the Riaño mountain regional park, at the start of the Picos de Europa.

Fromista

🛏️ Suggested base : Fromista
★★★☆☆
1

Frómista, 800 inhabitants, sits on the flat land of Tierra de Campos—the Castilian grain-growing plateau where the sky fills three-quarters of the landscape. A stop on the Camino Francés since the 12th-century Codex Calixtinus, the village has lived off passing pilgrims for a thousand years. The main street, Calle Mayor, is actually the Camino de Santiago itself: yellow arrows painted on the ground, shells in the paving, pilgrim guesthouses, municipal hostels. A village that probably wouldn't have survived the 20th century if the Camino hadn't been revived in the 1980s.

⏱ 30 min
2

The Church of San Martín de Frómista is the reason to stop here. Founded in 1066 by Doña Mayor, Countess of Castile and widow of Sancho the Great of Navarre, as part of a Benedictine monastery. The building seen today dates from around 1100. A basilican plan with a barrel-vaulted nave and two barrel-vaulted aisles, three circular apses, a polygonal lantern tower above the transept crossing, and two circular towers flanking the façade. The purity of its geometric form makes it the prototype of Castilian Romanesque.

⏱ 75 min
3

Inside San Martín, the capitals hold your attention. More than fifty, carved with fantastic animals, biblical scenes, and representations of medieval vices and virtues. The two-tailed mermaid, the miser strangled by his purse, contorted acrobats—the complete iconography of the 11th-century Romanesque imagination. Above the altar, a late-13th-century Gothic sculpture of Christ crucified, in polychrome wood, preserved in place. The restoration carried out between 1894 and 1904 by architect Aníbal Álvarez removed all later additions to restore the building's original Romanesque form—a controversial decision at the time, since endorsed.

⏱ 45 min
4

The Canal de Castilla runs through Frómista—an 18th-century hydraulic engineering project designed to transport Castilian wheat to the northern ports. Launched in 1753 under Ferdinand VI, completed only a century later, and already made obsolete by the arrival of the railroad. But the locks and towpaths remain. In Frómista, the four-stage lock—a 14-meter change in level spread across four chambers—is one of the most spectacular along the route. A walk along the canal, a tourist barge in season.

⏱ 45 min

Activities

Dinner in the village, simple but authentic—Frómista isn't a gastronomic hotspot, but its Castilian cuisine is worth the detour. Mesón Hostería de los Palmeros (a converted 12th-century hospital for pilgrims) or Restaurante San Telmo: lechazo asado (oven-roasted suckling lamb), morcilla de Burgos, queso de Burgos, wines from nearby Cigales and Ribera del Duero. Allow €25 per person. The atmosphere is that of a pilgrim village: unpretentious, welcoming, with scallop shells carved into the woodwork.

⏱ 90 min

A quiet evening in the village. About 65 km north of Frómista, a 45-minute drive, Aguilar de Campoo has the world's greatest concentration of Romanesque art—more than one hundred 11th–12th-century Romanesque churches in the north of the province. If you have time, detour to the Monastery of Santa María la Real in Aguilar (12th century, partly converted into a hotel and school of Romanesque studies), another major stop with exceptional heritage.

Burgos

🛏️ Suggested base : Burgos
★★★★★
1

Burgos, the historic capital of Castile from the 11th to the 15th century, birthplace of El Cid Campeador (around 1043). A city of 175,000 inhabitants crossed by the Arlanzón, dominated by its cathedral, which catches your eye from everywhere. The historic entrance, Arco de Santa María, is a 14th-century fortified gate remodeled in the 16th century as a Renaissance triumphal arch, with six niches housing statues of El Cid, King Charles V and the city's founders. The gateway into the old town.

⏱ 45 min
2

Burgos Cathedral. Construction began in 1221 on the orders of King Ferdinand III and Bishop Maurice (an Englishman educated in Paris), and was completed in 1567. The first Spanish Gothic building to imitate the cathedrals of Île-de-France directly—Reims, Bourges, Paris. It's Spain's only cathedral listed by UNESCO independently of its historic center (1984). 84 by 59 meters, two 84 m spires, 19 side chapels. Inside: the tomb of El Cid and Doña Jimena under the central dome (transferred in 1921), the spectacular Chapel of the Constable, the Golden Staircase by Diego de Siloé.

⏱ 120 min
3

A cathedral curiosity: the Papamoscas—the “flycatcher”. A human-shaped automaton perched 15 meters up in the central nave, which opens its mouth wide whenever the hour strikes and moves its arm to pull the rope. Operating since the 16th century. Beside it, a smaller automaton, the Martinillo, marks the quarter hours. Look up on the hour: the giant jaw opens, a little bell rings; it's mechanical, ridiculous, and fascinating. Especially for children.

⏱ 30 min
4

The Museum of Human Evolution, on the other bank of the Arlanzón, is one of Europe's best archaeology museums. Inaugurated in 2010, it presents discoveries from the Atapuerca site—15 km away—where Europe's oldest human remains were found in 1994 (1.2 million years for Homo antecessor). A bright building by Juan Navarro Baldeweg, with three floors, reconstructions, and original fossils including Miguelón's skull. Children can touch replicas. Allow 2 hours.

Burgos

🛏️ Suggested base : Burgos
★★★★★
1

The Cartuja de Miraflores, 4 km from the center along a walking route beside the Arlanzón, is one of Burgos's best-kept secrets. A former royal hunting lodge, converted into a Carthusian monastery in 1442 by John II of Castile, completed in 1488 by his daughter Isabella the Catholic. Three treasures inside: the main altarpiece carved by Gil de Siloé (1496–1499), decorated in color with the first gold brought from America (an offering from Isabella after Columbus's return); the double tomb of John II and Isabella of Portugal—alabaster, an eight-pointed star, over 200 carved figures; the tomb of Infante Alfonso. Free entry, donations welcome.

⏱ 90 min
2

The Monastery of Las Huelgas, 1.5 km west of the center, was founded in 1187 by Eleanor of England (wife of Alfonso VIII of Castile, daughter of Eleanor of Aquitaine). A Cistercian convent reserved for the Castilian aristocracy, whose abbess long held a status approaching that of a bishop—judicial authority over 60 villages. Exceptional architecture: a transitional Romanesque-Gothic church, a Mudéjar cloister, the Chapel of the Assumption with the canopy beneath which Saint Ferdinand was knighted in 1219. A royal tomb that yielded a unique collection of embroidered medieval clothing (12th–14th centuries) in the 20th century.

⏱ 90 min
3

Burgos Castle stands at the top of the hill overlooking the cathedral, a 15-minute climb. Built in the 9th century by Diego Rodríguez Porcelos, the founder of Burgos, it was devastated in 1813 when Napoleon blew up its defenses to prevent their reuse. Following recent restoration, there is not much architecture to see, but the view over the cathedral and the Castilian plain is unbeatable. Combine it with the Mirador del Castillo.

⏱ 60 min

Activities

An excursion to the Sierra de Atapuerca, 15 km from Burgos. Archaeological excavations active since 1978 have yielded Europe's oldest human fossils: Homo antecessor (1.2 million years), Homo heidelbergensis (430,000 years). A guided tour of the site (railroad trench, Sima de los Huesos), with scientific explanations, flint-knapping demonstrations, and access to replicas of caves with prehistoric art. It pairs well with the Museum of Human Evolution visited the day before: the material and its context.

⏱ 240 min

An alternative: an excursion to Ribera del Duero, 50 km south. One of Spain's three great red-wine appellations alongside Rioja and Priorat—tempranillo grapes, long aging, powerful structure. Emblematic wineries: Vega Sicilia (reserve well in advance, Spain's wine icon, wines at €600), Pingus (legendary, virtually inaccessible), Bodegas Protos (tours open to the public, an immense cellar dug into the hillside in Peñafiel). Peñafiel Castle (13th century) also houses a Wine Museum. Allow half a day.

Valladolid

🛏️ Suggested base : Valladolid
★★★★☆
1

Valladolid, the final stop. 300,000 inhabitants, capital of Castile and León, but a city often underestimated. Yet it was Spain's capital under Philip II and Philip III (1601–1606), the place where Cervantes published the first edition of Don Quixote in 1605, the city where Christopher Columbus died in 1506, and the site of the Catholic Monarchs' marriage in 1469. Plaza Mayor (1561), rebuilt after the fire of that same year, is one of Spain's oldest enclosed main squares and served as a model for Madrid and Salamanca. Red façades, white arcades, a lively square.

⏱ 60 min
2

The National Museum of Sculpture, in the Colegio de San Gregorio, is probably the finest museum in Castile and León—and one of northern Spain's least known. The college's Isabelline Gothic façade (1488–1496), carved like stone lace, is a work of art in itself. The collection: the world's most complete collection of Spanish polychrome sculpture from the 15th to the 18th century. Berruguete, Juan de Juni, Gregorio Fernández—the three giants of Castilian Spanish baroque. The raw realism of Christ on the cross, hyperrealistic expressions, preserved polychrome decoration. Allow 2 hours.

⏱ 120 min
3

The Cervantes House Museum, on Calle Rastro. The house where the writer lived from 1604 to 1606 with his family—his wife, sisters, and daughter born outside marriage. Here he corrected the proofs of the first edition of Don Quixote, published in January 1605. A small two-story house typical of 17th-century Valladolid, refurnished in period style by the Hispanic Society of America (Archer Huntington, American patron). Free on Sundays, modest but evocative. Steps from Plaza Mayor.

⏱ 60 min
4

The Church of San Pablo, on Plaza San Pablo, has a Plateresque façade resembling an altarpiece, dating from 1486–1492 and considered one of Spain's most extraordinary—four levels of sculpture, biblical scenes, coats of arms. The work of Simón de Colonia. Philip II was baptized here in 1527 (his father Charles V was in Valladolid). Right beside it, the Royal Palace (16th century), where the court temporarily settled between 1601 and 1606. Inside the church, an altarpiece by Pedro de Mena.

Valladolid Cathedral tells a story of incompletion. Designed in the 1580s by Juan de Herrera—the architect of El Escorial—to be Spain's largest cathedral.
